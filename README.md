# Autonomic Nervous System Fight-or-Flight Response Mechanisms — Deep Research Report

**Date researched:** 16 November 2026 | **Method:** web-index searches + direct text fetch of StatPearls and PMC open-access reviews (verified live where noted). No live browser login, no paywalled primary data accessed.
**Evidence flags used throughout:** **[ESTABLISHED]** = textbook / replicated, multiple independent reviews agree; **[EMERGING]** = supported by recent / smaller human or mainly animal evidence, not yet consensus; **[DEBATED]** = active scientific dispute, do not present as fact; **[TEXTBOOK]** = standard physiology teaching, corroborated by StatPearls/PMC reviews cited, but not traced to a single primary experiment in this run.

## Summary

Fight-or-flight is not one reflex but a **two-speed system**: (1) a **seconds-fast sympatho-adrenomedullary (SAM) axis** — central threat circuits → sympathetic preganglionic → postganglionic norepinephrine (NE) + adrenal-medullary epinephrine (Epi) → α/β-adrenergic GPCR signalling — and (2) a **minutes-to-hours hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis** — paraventricular nucleus (PVN) corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) → pituitary adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) → adrenal-cortex cortisol — that sustains, then, by negative feedback, terminates the response [1,2]. Central control is distributed in Benarroch's **central autonomic network (CAN)** — prefrontal/insular/anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala, hypothalamus, periaqueductal gray (PAG), locus coeruleus (LC), nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS), rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM) — not a single "fight-or-flight centre" [6]. Peripheral effects are determined less by the transmitter than by **which receptor subtype a tissue expresses** (α1-Gq, α2-Gi, β1/β2/β3-Gs) [3,4,5]. Termination is by NE reuptake (NET, >90% at cardiac synapses in one review), enzymatic degradation by monoamine oxidase (MAO) and catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT), cortisol feedback, and parasympathetic (vagal) rebound [4,9,10]. Chronic dysregulation is linked to PTSD, anxiety/panic, hypertension and Takotsubo syndrome, but causal direction and individual variation remain incompletely resolved [1,15,16]. **Polyvagal theory is DEBATED, not established** — its freeze/shutdown anatomy is contested by a 2023 systematic evaluation and a 2026 multi-author critique, with a published rebuttal from Porges [18,19].

## Findings

### 1. Anatomy and organization — sympathetic vs parasympathetic, pre/postganglionic, chain ganglia, adrenal medulla, craniosacral vs thoracolumbar

**[ESTABLISHED]** The autonomic nervous system (ANS) regulates involuntary processes — heart rate, blood pressure, respiration, digestion, sexual arousal — via three divisions: sympathetic, parasympathetic and enteric. Both sympathetic and parasympathetic efferents use a **two-neuron chain: preganglionic neuron (cell body in CNS) → ganglion → postganglionic neuron (cell body in periphery) → target tissue** [2,3]. All preganglionic fibres release acetylcholine (ACh) onto **nicotinic** receptors on postganglionic neurons, in both divisions [2,3].

| Feature | Sympathetic ("fight or flight") | Parasympathetic ("rest and digest") |
|---|---|---|
| Outflow **[ESTABLISHED]** | **Thoracolumbar: T1–L2** (some sources L3), cell bodies in intermediolateral column / lateral horn; fibres exit via anterior roots → white rami communicantes → sympathetic trunk [2,3] | **Craniosacral: CN III, VII, IX, X + S2–S4**; vagus (CN X) carries ~75% of parasympathetic fibres [2,3] |
| Ganglia | **Paravertebral (sympathetic chain/trunk)** close to cord + **prevertebral/collateral** (coeliac, superior/inferior mesenteric); short preganglionic, long postganglionic [2,3] | Terminal ganglia **near or within target organs**; long preganglionic, short postganglionic [2,3] |
| Postganglionic transmitter | Mostly **norepinephrine** onto adrenergic receptors; **exception: sweat glands (eccrine) and some skeletal-muscle vessels use ACh / muscarinic** [2,3] | **ACh** onto **muscarinic** receptors throughout [2,3] |
| Scope | Widespread, divergent — one preganglionic fibre can synapse on many postganglionics in the chain, enabling near-simultaneous whole-body discharge; innervates nearly every tissue [2] | Discrete, localised — suited to organ-specific control [2,3] |

**Adrenal medulla [ESTABLISHED]:** a modified sympathetic ganglion. Preganglionic splanchnic fibres synapse **directly on chromaffin cells** (no postganglionic neuron), which release catecholamines into blood — predominantly epinephrine (~80%) with norepinephrine (~20%) in standard teaching [1,4,10]. This is why circulating Epi reaches tissues with no direct sympathetic innervation (e.g. liver, metabolic tissues) and prolongs the response for minutes after neural firing stops. Sympathetic neurons themselves **cannot synthesise epinephrine** — they lack phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase (PNMT), present only in adrenal medulla (and a few CNS sites) [4].

**Enteric division:** intrinsic gut plexuses (myenteric/Auerbach, submucosal/Meissner) run local reflexes, modulated by both divisions — functionally a three-or-more-neuron chain where sympathetic/parasympathetic fibres synapse onto enteric neurons [2].

**Nuance:** the short-vs-long fibre rule is a teaching simplification; apart from the vagus, the ratio varies by region [search result, easyanatomylab/OpenStax summary]. Dual innervation with opposing effects is the rule for most organs, but some targets are sympathetic-only (e.g. most blood vessels, sweat glands, arrector pili) or parasympathetic-only (e.g. lacrimal glands) [2,3].

### 2. Central control — hypothalamus, amygdala, PAG, locus coeruleus, NTS, RVLM, prefrontal cortex

**[ESTABLISHED — framework]** Control is hierarchical and distributed: Benarroch's **central autonomic network (CAN)**, ascending: caudal ventrolateral medulla (CVLM) → NTS → dorsal motor nucleus of vagus / nucleus ambiguus → RVLM → raphe nuclei → locus coeruleus → PAG → parabrachial complex → PVN → amygdala/hippocampus → insular, anterior cingulate and prefrontal (orbitofrontal) cortex [6]. Dum et al. (2016, cited in [6]) confirmed direct cortical connections to the adrenal medulla, dispelling the idea the ANS is purely involuntary/unconscious.

- **Hypothalamus — PVN and lateral hypothalamus [ESTABLISHED]:** PVN is the integrator and the HPA origin: parvocellular PVN neurons release CRH (plus vasopressin/oxytocin as co-factors) into the hypophysial portal system [1,7]. PVN also contains pre-autonomic neurons projecting directly to brainstem/spinal sympathetic centres. Lateral hypothalamus is classically the "defence / sympathetic" hypothalamic area; stimulation produces the full fight-or-flight pattern (tachycardia, hypertension, pupillary dilation). **[EMERGING]** precise human PVN subnuclear mapping is largely extrapolated from rodents.
- **Amygdala — central nucleus (CeA) [ESTABLISHED in animals, EMERGING in human causal detail]:** Basolateral amygdala (BLA) receives polymodal cortical (insula, prefrontal) and sensory input, assigns threat valence, and relays to CeA and bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST). CeA is the main output nucleus: projections to hypothalamus, parabrachial nucleus, PAG and RVLM enable rapid HR/BP adjustment during threat [6,7]. CeA also synthesises/releases CRF into the locus coeruleus, driving NE release [7]. Fear activates CeA (+BNST); anxiety engages BNST more sustainedly — a distinction supported in animals, less cleanly separable in humans.
- **Periaqueductal gray (PAG) [ESTABLISHED in animals, EMERGING in humans]:** Midbrain tube around the cerebral aqueduct, organised in longitudinal columns: **dorsolateral/lateral (dl/lPAG) → active coping (fight/flight): tachycardia, hypertension, blood redistributed to limbs, fast non-opioid analgesia; ventrolateral (vlPAG) → passive coping (freeze/disengagement): bradycardia/hypotension, quiescence, hyporeactivity, longer-term opioid-mediated analgesia** [8]. Human 7-T fMRI (Linnman et al., PNAS 2013) found aversive-image activation segregated into rostral lateral/dorsomedial and caudal ventrolateral PAG subregions [8]. vlPAG and dlPAG are mutually inhibitory via GABAergic interneurons [8].
- **Locus coeruleus (LC) [ESTABLISHED]:** Pontine nucleus, principal source of NE in the brain. Aston-Jones & Cohen (2005) integrative theory: tonic firing = baseline arousal/vigilance, phasic bursts = orienting to salient stimuli; LC-NE optimises gain/performance [20]. LC is driven by amygdala/PAG/hypothalamus, projects widely to cortex, hippocampus, amygdala and spinal cord, and drives PVN-HPA activation; CRF in LC increases forebrain NE [7]. Pupil diameter closely tracks LC firing — the basis for pupillometry ( §9) [20,21].
- **Nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) [ESTABLISHED]:** Primary visceral sensory nucleus, receiving vagal/glossopharyngeal afferents (baroreceptors in aortic arch/carotid sinus, chemoreceptors, gut). Baroreflex arc: ↑BP → baroreceptor afferents → NTS → CVLM (GABA) → **inhibits RVLM** → ↓ sympathetic outflow; simultaneously NTS activates cardiovagal neurons in nucleus ambiguus → ↓HR [6,9]. On standing/baroreceptor unloading, the reverse disinhibits RVLM.
- **Rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM) [ESTABLISHED]:** Final common sympathetic efferent hub for vasomotor tone. Tonic RVLM activity maintains baseline BP; its output to intermediolateral spinal preganglionics increases myocardial contractility, HR and peripheral vasoconstriction [6]. Conversely nucleus ambiguus + dorsal motor nucleus of vagus are the cardiac parasympathetic sources [6].
- **Prefrontal cortex — top-down regulation [ESTABLISHED as a circuit, EMERGING as a therapeutic target]:** Medial/ventromedial PFC and anterior cingulate exert inhibitory top-down control over amygdala/CeA and hypothalamus; failure of PFC inhibition of a hyper-reactive amygdala is a leading model of PTSD hyperarousal [15]. Higher resting vagally-mediated HRV is linked to PFC executive functions (attention, emotion regulation) [9]. Insula provides interoceptive awareness (body-state sensing) feeding into this loop [6]. Slow breathing / PFC engagement can increase RSA even during high sympathetic activity — the two branches are **not** a zero-sum teeter-totter [9].

**Sequence in a threat (simplified, [ESTABLISHED] as a model):** sensory/threat appraisal (BLA) → CeA → (a) PVN/lateral hypothalamus → sympathetic outflow + HPA, (b) PAG (dl/l = fight/flight, vl = freeze), (c) LC → brain-wide NE arousal, (d) RVLM → spinal sympathetic surge; NTS/baroreflex and PFC continuously modulate the gain.

### 3. Neurotransmitters and receptors — ACh, NE, Epi, α/β subtypes, co-transmitters

**[ESTABLISHED]** All adrenergic receptors are **G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs)**. Ahlquist (1948) first distinguished α vs β pharmacologically.

| Receptor | G protein / 2nd messenger | Main locations | Main effect **[ESTABLISHED]** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **α1** (α1A/B/D) | **Gq → PLC → IP3 + DAG → ↑cytosolic Ca²⁺, PKC** | Vascular smooth muscle (skin, mucosa, gut, kidney), iris radial muscle, GI/urinary sphincters, liver | **Vasoconstriction** (↑ peripheral resistance, ↑BP), **mydriasis** (pupil dilation), sphincter contraction, hepatic glycogenolysis [3,4,5] |
| **α2** (α2A/B/C) | **Gi → ↓cAMP, ↓PKA**; also ↓Ca²⁺ channel / ↑K⁺ channel | Presynaptic sympathetic terminals, brainstem (NTS), vascular smooth muscle, platelets, pancreatic β-cells | **Presynaptic negative feedback: inhibits further NE release**; central ↓ sympathetic outflow (clonidine target); postsynaptic vasoconstriction; platelet aggregation; **inhibits insulin secretion** [3,4,5] |
| **β1** | **Gs → adenylyl cyclase → ↑cAMP → PKA** | Heart (SA/AV node, myocardium), juxtaglomerular kidney cells | **↑HR (chronotropy), ↑contractility (inotropy), ↑AV conduction (dromotropy), ↑relaxation (lusitropy); ↑renin release** [3,4,5] |
| **β2** | **Gs → ↑cAMP → PKA** | Bronchial smooth muscle, skeletal-muscle vessels, liver, uterus, GI/GU smooth muscle, skeletal muscle | **Bronchodilation**, skeletal-muscle **vasodilation**, hepatic **glycogenolysis**, ↓GI motility, uterine/bladder relaxation, ↑K⁺ uptake into muscle [3,4,5] |
| **β3** | **Gs → ↑cAMP** | White/brown adipose tissue, bladder detrusor | **Lipolysis** (free fatty acids + glycerol), thermogenesis in brown fat, detrusor relaxation [3,4,5] |

**Ligand affinities [ESTABLISHED]:** Epi stimulates all α and β subtypes roughly equally; NE has high affinity for α1 = α2 and β1, but **very low affinity for β2** — hence NE is a potent vasoconstrictor/pressor with no bronchodilation, while Epi raises systolic BP but may lower diastolic BP / total peripheral resistance via β2 vasodilation [5]. This explains why Epi (not NE) is first-line in anaphylaxis (α1 vasoconstriction + β1 cardiac + β2 bronchodilation).

**Cholinergic [ESTABLISHED]:** ACh at ganglia = **nicotinic (Nn, ligand-gated Na⁺/K⁺ channel, fast)**; ACh at parasympathetic targets and at sympathetic sweat glands = **muscarinic (M1–M5, GPCR; cardiac M2 = Gi → ↓HR, ↓cAMP)**. Acetylcholinesterase terminates ACh in milliseconds by hydrolysis — far faster than catecholamine clearance (§7) [4].

**Co-transmitters [ESTABLISHED in animals, EMERGING for human quantitative contribution]:** NE, **neuropeptide Y (NPY)** and **ATP** are co-stored in and co-released from sympathetic nerves [11]. ATP (small vesicles, P2X receptors) = **fast** vasoconstriction; NE (α1) = **slow** phase; NPY (large dense-core vesicles, Y1 receptors) = **very slow**, prolonged vasoconstriction and potentiation of NE/ATP [11]. Each also acts prejunctionally to inhibit release of all three (NE→α2, NPY→Y2, ATP→P2Y). Lower stimulation frequencies release ATP+NE vesicles; **high-frequency (stress-level) firing is required to release NPY vesicles** — so NPY is disproportionately a stress co-transmitter [11]. NPY also has central anxiolytic/resilience roles in some models **[EMERGING, animal-heavy]**. Other co-modulators: angiotensin II, serotonin, histamine, endothelin, prostaglandins, nitric oxide (NO) — NO both antagonises vasoconstriction and inhibits catecholamine release [11].

### 4. HPA axis and its interaction with the sympathetic response

**[ESTABLISHED]** Two axes, two timescales [1,2]:

|  | SAM axis | HPA axis |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | **Seconds** — neural, no hormonal relay | **Minutes; cortisol peak ~15–30 min** after stressor onset |
| Route | Hypothalamus → sympathetic nerves → **adrenal medulla → Epi/NE into blood** | **PVN CRH → portal blood → anterior pituitary ACTH → blood → adrenal cortex → cortisol** |
| Duration | Minutes (catecholamine half-life ~1–3 min) | Minutes to hours; genomic cortisol effects last longer |
| Function | Immediate action: ↑HR/CO/BP, bronchodilation, glucose mobilisation, blood to muscle | Sustained coping: maintain glucose (gluconeogenesis), vascular responsiveness to catecholamines, modulate immunity/inflammation, consolidate emotional memory |

**Cascade detail [ESTABLISHED]:** Parvocellular PVN neurons release 41-amino-acid **CRH** (+ arginine vasopressin, which potentiates ACTH release). CRH acts mainly via **CRH-R1** (widely distributed in brain; key for ACTH release) vs CRH-R2 (peripheral: skeletal muscle, GI tract, heart; subcortical brain) [1]. CRH-binding protein (CRH-BP) binds 40–60% of CRH in brain and rises with stress, limiting CRH–R1 interaction [1]. ACTH stimulates adrenal cortex (zona fasciculata) to synthesise/release **cortisol**; ~80% of serum cortisol is bound to cortisol-binding globulin, ~10% to albumin, the unbound fraction being active [1]. Cortisol is interconverted with inactive cortisone by 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases [1].

**Interaction [ESTABLISHED in outline, EMERGING in detail]:** The axes are not independent. LC-NE drives PVN CRH release; CRF in LC increases forebrain NE; glucocorticoids and catecholamines modulate each other — in acute stress cortisol helps normalise catecholamine-induced arousal in limbic structures, while **high arousal + low glucocorticoid** may promote undifferentiated fight/flight [7,15]. Cortisol also **permissively maintains vascular responsiveness** to catecholamines (without cortisol, vessels become refractory) and potentiates catecholamine metabolic effects. PACAP (pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide) modulates CRH production and catecholamine secretion at multiple HPA levels **[EMERGING]** [1].

**Negative feedback [ESTABLISHED]:** Rising cortisol suppresses CRH (hypothalamus) and ACTH (pituitary) — long-loop feedback — and acts via glucocorticoid receptors in hippocampus, which signals the hypothalamus to stand down [1,2]. The system is therefore self-limiting in acute stress. **Chronic stress does not uniformly raise cortisol:** some people with depression, PTSD or burnout show **blunted/flattened diurnal cortisol** or receptor desensitisation, not elevation [1,2,15] — a common oversimplification to avoid.

### 5. Peripheral physiological effects by organ system

**[ESTABLISHED]** — summarised from StatPearls stress physiology [1] and adrenergic reviews [3,5]:

- **Cardiovascular:** β1 → ↑HR, ↑contractility, ↑AV conduction → ↑cardiac output; α1 → vasoconstriction in skin, mucosa, gut, kidney (↑ total peripheral resistance, ↑BP) **vs** β2 → vasodilation in skeletal muscle and heart — net effect is **redistribution**: blood away from gut/skin, toward skeletal muscle, heart and brain. Epi ↑systolic BP markedly, diastolic variable; NE ↑both systolic and diastolic BP [5].
- **Respiratory:** β2 → bronchodilation, ↑ rate/depth of breathing, ↑ oxygen intake; acute stress can also produce rapid shallow breathing / airway hyperresponsiveness in susceptible people (asthma) [1].
- **Metabolic:** β2/α1 (liver) → **glycogenolysis**; cortisol + catecholamines → **gluconeogenesis**; β3 (adipose) → **lipolysis** → free fatty acids + glycerol; α2 on pancreatic β-cells → **insulin suppression** (so glucose stays available to muscle/brain); ↑ oxygen consumption and thermogenesis (basal metabolic rate ↑ ~10–15% in textbook accounts). Net: transient stress hyperglycaemia.
- **Gastrointestinal:** α-adrenergic → delayed gastric emptying, ↓ intestinal motility/transit, α-mediated gut vasoconstriction → ↓ secretions/absorption; stress impairs mucosal barrier integrity (↑ permeability) and alters gut microbiota via the gut–brain axis [1]. Diarrhoea or constipation can both occur depending on context/rebound.
- **Pupillary / eye:** α1 on iris radial (dilator) muscle → **mydriasis**; parasympathetic (M3, sphincter) constricts — the balance sets pupil size [3,5].
- **Sweat glands / skin:** Sympathetic **cholinergic** fibres to eccrine glands → sweating (thermoregulation, also palmar "stress sweating"); α1 cutaneous vasoconstriction → pale/cool skin; arrector pili (α1) → piloerection.
- **Urinary / reproductive:** β2 detrusor relaxation + α1 sphincter contraction → urinary retention; chronic stress suppresses GnRH → ↓LH/FSH → menstrual irregularity/anovulation, ↓ testosterone, erectile dysfunction, ↓ sperm quality [1].
- **Immune modulation [EMERGING / complex, not simply "suppression"]:** Acute catecholamines redistribute leukocytes (mobilisation to blood, then trafficking to tissues) and can enhance some innate responses; **chronic** cortisol + sympathetic activation suppresses innate/adaptive immunity (↓ pro-inflammatory cytokines acutely, ↓ lymphocyte activity) yet paradoxically promotes systemic low-grade inflammation (↑ CRP, IL-6) in chronic stress — the acute-vs-chronic distinction is essential [1,15]. Stress also alters inflammatory cytokines in airways and gut [1].

### 6. Molecular / cellular signalling — GPCR cascades, cAMP/PKA, calcium

**[ESTABLISHED]**

- **β1/β2/β3 (Gs):** Catecholamine → receptor conformational change → Gsα (GTP-bound) → **adenylyl cyclase → ATP → cAMP (second messenger) → protein kinase A (PKA)** → phosphorylation of L-type Ca²⁺ channels (↑ Ca²⁺ influx), phospholamban (↑ sarcoplasmic-reticulum Ca²⁺ uptake → faster relaxation/lusitropy), ryanodine receptors, troponin I and other targets → ↑HR, ↑contractility, ↑conduction [4,5]. In liver/adipose, PKA phosphorylates glycogen phosphorylase kinase / hormone-sensitive lipase → glycogenolysis / lipolysis.
- **α1 (Gq):** → phospholipase C → PIP2 → **IP3 (releases Ca²⁺ from sarcoplasmic reticulum) + DAG (activates PKC)** → smooth-muscle contraction (vasoconstriction, mydriasis) [4,5].
- **α2 (Gi):** → **↓ adenylyl cyclase → ↓cAMP → ↓PKA**, plus modulation of K⁺/Ca²⁺ channels → presynaptic inhibition of transmitter release [4,5].
- **Muscarinic M2 (Gi, cardiac parasympathetic):** ↓cAMP, opens K⁺ channels (IKACh) → hyperpolarisation, ↓HR — and it acts in **<1 s**, vs sympathetic effects taking **>5 s**, which is why vagal withdrawal can raise HR almost instantly and vagal reactivation can drop it fast [9].
- **Desensitisation / termination at receptor level [ESTABLISHED]:** Sustained catecholamine exposure → GPCR kinase (GRK)-mediated phosphorylation → β-arrestin binding → receptor internalisation/down-regulation. This underlies tolerance in chronic sympathetic overdrive and is a mechanism in heart failure **[EMERGING as a therapeutic target]**.
- **Other second messengers:** Ca²⁺ itself (via IP3 and L-type channels), DAG/PKC, and — for ATP co-transmission — P2X ligand-gated cation channels (fast depolarisation, excitatory junction potentials) [11].

### 7. Termination and recovery — parasympathetic rebound, catecholamine clearance, vagal tone, HRV

**[ESTABLISHED]**

- **Catecholamine clearance:** (i) **Neuronal reuptake via NET (norepinephrine transporter, SLC6A2, "uptake-1") is the dominant route for NE** — >90% of NE is cleared from the cardiac synaptic cleft by NET in one review, recycling it into the presynaptic terminal [4,10]; a small fraction (~5%) enters postsynaptic cells via OCT3/extraneuronal transporter [10]. (ii) **Enzymatic degradation: MAO** (MAO-A/B, deaminates the amine → DHPG/DOPAC) and **COMT** (O-methylates the catechol → normetanephrine/metanephrine) in local tissues and liver; circulating Epi is mainly cleared this way [4]. Circulating Epi half-life is **~1–3 min (commonly cited ~2 min)** [10]. NET dysfunction (genetic or acquired) is implicated in hyperadrenergic POTS (§8).
- **ACh termination is far faster:** acetylcholinesterase hydrolysis in the synaptic cleft [4] — a key reason parasympathetic effects are brief and precisely timed.
- **Parasympathetic rebound [ESTABLISHED as a phenomenon, EMERGING as a quantified mechanism]:** After high stress, PNS activity can overshoot baseline — Shaffer & Ginsberg note increased nighttime gastric activity and asthma symptoms after stress as examples [9]. HR recovery after exercise involves **PNS reactivation while SNS activity remains elevated** — the branches co-activate, not simply switch [9]. Slow breathing can engage both branches and increase respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA) during high SNS activity [9].
- **Vagal tone / HRV as a readout [ESTABLISHED with caveats]:** HRV = fluctuation in interbeat intervals [9]. **RMSSD and pNN50 (time-domain) and HF power (0.15–0.40 Hz) index cardiac vagal/parasympathetic activity**; SDNN reflects total variability (both branches); **LF power (0.04–0.15 Hz) is mixed sympathetic + parasympathetic, and the LF/HF ratio does NOT reliably index "sympathovagal balance"** — it will not always do so, especially outside controlled conditions [9]. 24-h, short-term (~5 min) and ultra-short-term (<5 min) norms are **not interchangeable**; age, sex, posture, breathing rate and recording length all shift values [9]. Higher HRV is not always better (e.g. atrial fibrillation inflates HRV and predicts mortality) [9]. At rest PNS predominates (~75 bpm) and can slow the heart to 20–30 bpm or briefly stop it ("accentuated antagonism") [9].

### 8. Dysregulation and clinical relevance

- **Chronic stress [ESTABLISHED as association, EMERGING as causal mechanism]:** Sustained SNS + HPA activation → elevated catecholamines/cortisol (or, in some people, blunted cortisol), oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, dyslipidaemia → atherosclerosis and cardiovascular risk; also depression, anxiety, cognitive impairment [1]. McEwen's allostatic load model (chronic wear-and-tear) is the dominant framework **[ESTABLISHED as a model, EMERGING in its biomarker quantification]**.
- **PTSD [ESTABLISHED]:** Hyperarousal cluster (hypervigilance, exaggerated startle, intrusive memories) is linked to elevated baseline NE and exaggerated NE responses to trauma reminders, LC hyperactivity, and failure of PFC top-down inhibition of a hyper-reactive amygdala [15]. HPA findings are heterogeneous — often **low/flat cortisol** rather than high. Numbing/avoidance coexists with intermittent hyperarousal [15].
- **Anxiety disorders & panic attacks [ESTABLISHED in outline]:** Panic involves abrupt SAM surge (palpitations, dyspnoea, sweating, dizziness, fear) with LC-NE and amygdala-PAG involvement; hyperadrenergic POTS symptoms are physiologically almost identical to panic and are frequently misattributed to anxiety alone — a diagnostic trap noted in recent POTS reviews [16].
- **Dysautonomia / POTS [ESTABLISHED criteria, EMERGING mechanisms]:** POTS = chronic orthostatic intolerance + sustained HR rise **≥30 bpm within 10 min of standing (≥40 bpm in adolescents 12–19 y)** without orthostatic hypotension (≥20/10 mmHg drop) [16]. Affects an estimated 0.1–1% of the US population, predominantly women of childbearing age [16]. Mechanisms are heterogeneous: hypovolaemia, venous pooling, deconditioning, peripheral autonomic neuropathy, **hyperadrenergic state (standing NE ≥600 pg/mL, ↑BP on standing, in up to ~50% in some series)**, immune/autoantibody mechanisms [16]. A 2026 Frontiers review argues "dysautonomia" is often over-interpreted as autonomic *failure*, when in many patients the physiology is **compensatory** tachycardia from an intact baroreflex defending output against low preload/pooling [16] — **DEBATED framing, not settled**.
- **Hypertension [ESTABLISHED]:** Chronic sympathetic overdrive contributes via α1 vasoconstriction, β1 renin release, and NO-dependent modulation of sympathetic transmission being diminished in hypertension [11]. Sympathetic co-transmitter balance (NPY/ATP) is altered in hypertensive animals [11].
- **Takotsubo (stress) cardiomyopathy [ESTABLISHED as a clinical entity, EMERGING as to exact mechanism]:** Acute, typically reversible LV dysfunction (apical ballooning) mimicking ACS without obstructive coronary disease, after emotional or physical stress. Catecholamine surge **2–3× higher than in myocardial infarction** is the leading mechanism, causing myocardial stunning/microvascular dysfunction; apical vulnerability is attributed to higher β2-receptor density (β2 can switch Gs→Gi at very high Epi, producing negative inotropy — a leading but not proven explanation). International Takotsubo Registry (Templin et al., NEJM 2015, 1,750 patients): **89.9% women, mean age 67 y**, trigger in ~76% (emotional ≈ physical), in-hospital mortality 4.1%, comparable to ACS [17]. >90% postmenopausal women in most series; anxiety/depression in up to ~56%/48% in one 2025 review [17]. A 2026 Cureus editorial proposes a sequential sympathetic-stress-reflex + dive-reflex (parasympathetic bradycardia) conflict — **hypothesis, not established** [17].
- **Freeze response vs fight/flight [ESTABLISHED as a behaviour, EMERGING as to mechanism]:** Freezing (attentive immobility) is a real, adaptive defensive state — vlPAG-mediated, with co-activation of sympathetic and parasympathetic systems, bradycardia/hyporeactivity and opioid analgesia in animals [8]. In humans, emotional freezing/dissociation typically shows **no large HR drop — HR stays the same or dips slightly** — which is central to the polyvagal dispute below.
- **Polyvagal theory [DEBATED — do not present as established fact]:** Porges (1994/1995, elaborated 2011) proposed a three-level hierarchy: ventral vagal (nucleus ambiguus) = safety/social engagement (mammalian, newest); sympathetic = fight/flight; dorsal vagal (dorsal motor nucleus) = shutdown/freeze/dissociation (primitive). Grossman (2023, *Biol Psychol* 180:108589, doi:10.1016/j.biopsycho.2023.108589, PMID 37230290) evaluated all five premises and concluded each is **untenable or highly implausible**, arguing RSA (respiratory sinus arrhythmia) is an *index* of vagal activity, not vagal tone itself (a "category mistake"), and that the dorsal motor nucleus has not been shown to drive bradycardia/shutdown in mammals — ventral nucleus ambiguus accounts for virtually all vagally-mediated HR control [18]. In 2026, Grossman et al. ("Why the Polyvagal Theory Is Untenable", *Clin Neuropsychiatry*, 38–39 co-authors) restated this; Porges published a rebuttal in the same issue ("When A Critique Becomes Untenable"), arguing the critics attacked a straw-man version [19]. **Status: active, unresolved dispute. The *experiences* (freeze, shutdown, dissociation) are real and clinically important; the *specific dorsal-vagal anatomical mechanism and evolutionary hierarchy* are contested.** Slow-breathing / co-regulation interventions have independent evidence (e.g. a 2022 review of 223 studies found slow breathing raises vagally-mediated HRV) — that evidence does **not** validate polyvagal anatomy [19].

### 9. Measurement methods

| Method | What it measures | Strengths / limits **[ESTABLISHED unless noted]** |
|---|---|---|
| **Heart rate variability (HRV)** [9] | Interbeat-interval variation from ECG (gold standard) or PPG wearables | RMSSD/pNN50/HF = vagal; SDNN = total; LF = mixed; LF/HF ≠ clean balance index. Norms depend on recording length, age, sex, posture, breathing. Non-invasive, continuous, but confounded by respiration, movement, ectopy. |

# Appendix: GLP-1 and the Gut–Brain Axis — Connections to the Autonomic Fight-or-Flight System

*Added 16 November 2026. Reference numbers in this appendix (e.g. [1], [2]) refer to the appendix's own Sources list at its end, not to the main report's Sources.*

*Add-on section to an existing survey on autonomic fight-or-flight mechanisms*
*Research date: 16 November 2026. Evidence flags used throughout: **ESTABLISHED** = replicated in humans / multiple independent lines; **EMERGING** = consistent animal or small-human evidence, not yet replicated at scale; **DEBATED** = sources disagree or translation to humans is unresolved. All sources were read via the web index / full-text fetch on 16 Nov 2026 (not a live logged-in browser session); DOIs/PMIDs are given where the source itself supplied them. Regulatory status dated Nov 2026 changes fast — verify against the current FDA label before any clinical use.*

## Summary

GLP-1 sits at the exact junction the fight-or-flight survey needs: it is at once a gut hormone, a brainstem neuropeptide, and a drug target whose agonists measurably shift autonomic balance. The core, counter-intuitive finding is that **the peripheral and central GLP-1 systems are largely separate circuits, not one hormone acting everywhere** [1]. Gut-derived GLP-1 is degraded within 1–2 minutes and mostly never reaches the brain as a hormone; it signals mainly by activating vagal afferents and by reaching circumventricular organs that lack a blood–brain barrier [1,2]. Brain-derived GLP-1 comes from a small population of preproglucagon (PPG) neurons in the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) that project to hypothalamic, limbic and autonomic centres — including the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) CRH neurons that drive the HPA axis and brainstem catecholamine neurons that drive sympathetic outflow [1,3,4].

For fight-or-flight physiology this yields three actionable links:

1. **Central GLP-1 is a stress signal, not only a satiety signal.** In rodents, central GLP-1 activates the HPA axis (CRH → ACTH → corticosterone) and sympathetic outflow (higher heart rate and blood pressure, adrenal-medulla activation), and endogenous GLP-1 signalling is required for normal HPA responses to both psychogenic and systemic stressors [3,4,5].
2. **GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) raise resting heart rate by ~2–4 bpm on average** — a small, consistent, dose-related chronotropic effect with two mechanisms (direct sinoatrial-node GLP-1 receptors and increased sympathetic drive) — **yet the large outcome trials show fewer, not more, cardiovascular events** (semaglutide SELECT: MACE hazard ratio 0.80) [6,7,8]. The paradox is real and unresolved mechanistically.
3. **Stress runs the loop backwards.** Sympathetic activation and cortisol suppress gut motility and vagal tone, increase intestinal permeability, alter microbiota and (in rodent/dexamethasone models) reduce L-cell GLP-1 secretion — feeding altered vagal afferent input back to the NTS, PVN and amygdala [9,10,11]. Whether acute psychological stress changes gut GLP-1 secretion in humans is **not established** — a named gap, not a finding.

## 1. GLP-1 biology

### 1.1 Two sources, one gene, different products

**ESTABLISHED.** GLP-1 is a 30–31-amino-acid peptide cleaved from the 160-amino-acid precursor preproglucagon, encoded by *GCG* [1,12]. Tissue-specific prohormone-convertase processing yields different products in different tissues: glucagon in pancreatic α-cells, GLP-1 (plus GLP-2, oxyntomodulin and PYY co-secretion) in intestinal L-cells via PC1/3, and GLP-1 in brain PPG neurons [1,12].

- **Peripheral source — enteroendocrine L-cells.** L-cells are sparse enteroendocrine cells located largely in the distal small intestine and colon, with a smaller proximal small-intestinal population that may account for the rapid postprandial rise [1]. Secretion is biphasic: an early rise within 15–30 min (neuroendocrine/vagal and enteric signals, before nutrients reach distal L-cells) and a second peak at ~90–120 min from direct nutrient contact [12]. Secretagogues include glucose, amino acids and lipids; SCFAs act via GPR41/43 (see §2.4) [9,12].
- **Central source — PPG neurons of the NTS.** The primary brain source is preproglucagon-expressing neurons in the caudal NTS and intermediate reticular nucleus of the lower brainstem, with smaller populations in olfactory bulb, piriform cortex and lumbosacral spinal cord [1,3]. These neurons project widely — to the area postrema (AP) and dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus (DMV) in the dorsal vagal complex, hypothalamic arcuate (ARC), paraventricular (PVN/PVH), lateral and dorsomedial nuclei, and to reward regions (VTA, nucleus accumbens, lateral septum) [1,13]. Their projection pattern largely matches GLP-1 receptor distribution [3].
- **Pancreatic α-cell GLP-1** exists at much lower levels and may partially mediate the incretin effect, but is not the main systemic pool [1].

**ESTABLISHED — and a key corrective for the survey.** The peripheral and central systems are *at least largely disconnected*: NTS PPG neurons do **not** receive inputs from GLP-1-receptor-expressing vagal afferents; their major vagal input is from mechanosensitive oxytocin-receptor-expressing vagal afferents [1]. Deleting the preproglucagon gene from the gut almost completely eliminates circulating GLP-1 yet has **no effect on food intake or body weight**; conversely, global GLP-1-receptor knockout mice have normal body weight and food intake on chow and are *protected* from high-fat-diet-induced obesity [1]. Endogenous peripheral GLP-1 therefore has, at most, a limited role in long-term energy balance — a conclusion that overturns the simple "gut hormone → brain satiety" story [1].

### 1.2 DPP-4 degradation and half-life

**ESTABLISHED.** Native GLP-1(7–36)amide is cleaved at its N-terminus by dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) to inactive GLP-1(9–36)amide, with further degradation by neprilysin (neutral endopeptidase 24.11), plus renal clearance [2,14]. Consequences:

- Plasma half-life **~1–2 min** (often quoted as <2 min); only ~10–15% of secreted GLP-1 reaches the systemic circulation intact [2,12,14].
- Holst's formulation: most GLP-1 is inactivated by DPP-4 *before* reaching the systemic circulation, so it "acts predominantly via sensory afferent systems" rather than as a classical circulating hormone [2].
- This is precisely why GLP-1RAs had to be engineered DPP-4-resistant (fatty-acid acylation/albumin binding, or non-peptide small molecules): liraglutide t½ ≈13 h; semaglutide t½ ≈165–184 h (~7 days); tirzepatide ~5 days (weekly dosing) [12,15]. The long half-life is also why drug effects (including nausea and heart-rate elevation) persist for days, unlike the minutes-long endogenous signal.

## 2. Gut-to-brain signalling routes

Four parallel routes carry gut information to the brain; GLP-1 uses at least three of them. The vagus is the fastest and, for physiological GLP-1, the dominant route [1,2,9].

### 2.1 Vagal afferent pathway — nodose ganglion → NTS

**ESTABLISHED.** The vagus (CN X) is ~80% afferent and ~20% efferent; afferent cell bodies lie in the nodose (inferior) ganglion and terminate centrally in the NTS [9]. The circuit:

1. **Sensing at the gut wall.** Vagal afferent terminals in the lamina propria do not enter the lumen. They are activated (a) by enteroendocrine hormones — GLP-1, PYY, CCK — released when L/K cells sense nutrients/microbial metabolites; (b) by serotonin from enterochromaffin cells; (c) by cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α) during inflammation; and (d) by mechanical stretch [1,9]. GLP-1 increases cytosolic Ca²⁺ and evokes action potentials in vagal afferent neurons, and GLP-1R is expressed in nodose ganglion neurons [16].
2. **A newly recognised fast synapse — neuropod cells.** **ESTABLISHED (mouse).** A subset of enteroendocrine cells ("neuropod cells") extend basal processes that form true synapses with vagal afferents, releasing **glutamate** and transducing luminal signals in **milliseconds** — orders of magnitude faster than hormonal diffusion [17]. Kaelberer et al., *Science* 2018: "enteroendocrine cells synapse with vagal neurons to transduce gut luminal signals in milliseconds by using glutamate as a neurotransmitter… connects the intestinal lumen to the brainstem in one synapse" [17]. Whether GLP-1-containing L-cells themselves are neuropod cells, vs neighbouring enteroendocrine subtypes, is **EMERGING** — do not assert a "GLP-1 neuropod synapse" as established.
3. **NTS integration and ascent.** From the NTS, information ascends via (i) a visceral lemniscal pathway to thalamus/insular cortex (interoception) and (ii) a parabrachial pathway to hypothalamus (PVN, ARC), amygdala and locus coeruleus [9,16]. The NTS also houses the PPG neurons themselves, so gut vagal input and central GLP-1 output converge in the same nucleus — but, as noted in §1.1, PPG neurons are driven mainly by mechanosensitive (oxytocin-receptor) vagal afferents, not by GLP-1R vagal afferents [1].
4. **Vagal efferent return.** NTS → DMV → vagal efferents modulate gastric motility, secretion and the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway (acetylcholine → α7 nicotinic receptor on macrophages), which dampens peripheral inflammation and decreases intestinal permeability [9].

**Functional test.** GLP-1R signalling in vagal afferents is *required* for native GLP-1- and GLP-1RA-induced slowing of gastric emptying, but lentiviral GLP-1R knockdown in vagal afferents in rats had no effect on body weight or total daily intake (larger meals, fewer meals) [1]. Vagotomy/human vagal studies variably implicate vagal GLP-1R in glucose tolerance and the incretin effect [1] — **DEBATED**, do not present vagal mediation of weight loss as settled.

### 2.2 Humoral / endocrine route via circumventricular organs

**ESTABLISHED.** The area postrema (AP) and parts of the ARC lack a complete blood–brain barrier [1]. Circulating GLP-1 (the ~10% surviving DPP-4) and, far more effectively, long-acting GLP-1RAs can act directly on AP/NTS GLP-1R neurons, which project to NTS, ventrolateral medulla and parabrachial nucleus [1,16]. This is the route for drug-induced nausea/emesis (see §3.3) and a major route for drug-induced satiety: acutely administered liraglutide/semaglutide "do not access most deep brain regions" but act in/near circumventricular areas; at steady state some access PVH and DMH [1]. The humoral route is considered the *less likely* route for endogenous GLP-1's central effects, because of the 1–2 min half-life [12].

### 2.3 Enteric nervous system (ENS)

**ESTABLISHED.** The ENS (~100 million neurons, myenteric and submucosal plexuses) governs motility, secretion and local sensing semi-autonomously, in continuous two-way communication with the CNS via vagal and spinal afferents/efferents [9,18]. GLP-1R is expressed on myenteric neurons; GLP-1 activation there reduces antral contractions and delays gastric emptying via nitrergic and cAMP-dependent pathways [19]. A recently described GLP-1R-expressing intestinofugal neuron population promotes stomach distension and anorexia via a spinal afferent circuit [1]. **EMERGING:** whether ENS GLP-1R signalling contributes to stress-related dysmotility independently of vagal/central routes.

### 2.4 Gut microbiota metabolites and immune signalling

**ESTABLISHED as a framework; EMERGING in mechanistic detail for GLP-1 specifically.**

- **Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) — acetate, propionate, butyrate.** Produced by bacterial fermentation of dietary fibre. They stimulate L-cell GLP-1 (and PYY) release via GPR41/FFAR3 and GPR43/FFAR2, strengthen the epithelial barrier, are anti-inflammatory (NF-κB inhibition), and support blood–brain barrier integrity [9,10,20]. Stress reduces SCFA production, compromising mucosal repair [10].
- **Bile acids.** Microbially modified bile acids signal via FXR–FGF15/19 and TGR5; bile acids have also been reported to inhibit CRH release, providing a microbiota→HPA link [10]. **EMERGING.**
- **Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and cytokines.** Increased intestinal permeability ("leaky gut") allows LPS translocation into the circulation, driving low-grade inflammation (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) [9,10,20]. LPS/cytokines activate vagal afferents and, via circumventricular organs, central autonomic/HPA circuits; dysbiosis with reduced SCFA producers and higher LPS is associated with IBS/IBD and with mood symptoms, but causality in humans is **DEBATED** (mostly observational/correlational).
- **Other microbial signals.** Tryptophan metabolites/indoles, bacterial neurotransmitter production (GABA, serotonin precursors), and — a 2025 finding — TLR5⁺ colonic neuroglial/enteric neurons detecting bacterial flagellin within milliseconds and regulating feeding via PYY→vagal signalling [20]. Treat the flagellin result as **EMERGING** (single recent line of work).
- **Cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway.** Vagal efferent acetylcholine → α7nAChR on macrophages dampens inflammation; stress inhibits the vagus, removing this brake [9].

## 3. GLP-1 interaction with the autonomic fight-or-flight system

This is the section that most directly extends the existing survey. The unifying point: **GLP-1 signalling and sympathetic activation are not opposed systems — central GLP-1 *drives* sympathetic and HPA output, while peripheral GLP-1RA therapy *also* produces a small sympathomimetic-like heart-rate rise, even as it lowers blood pressure and cardiovascular events.**

### 3.1 Sympathetic outflow and heart rate

**ESTABLISHED (rodent mechanism) / ESTABLISHED (human heart-rate effect, size ~2–4 bpm).**

- **Rodent mechanism — Yamamoto et al. 2002** [5]: centrally *and* peripherally administered GLP-1R agonists dose-dependently increased blood pressure and heart rate in rats, induced c-Fos in the adrenal medulla and in medullary catecholamine neurons providing input to sympathetic preganglionic neurons, and rapidly activated tyrosine-hydroxylase transcription in brainstem catecholamine neurons. Conclusion: "the central GLP-1 system represents a regulator of sympathetic outflow leading to downstream activation of cardiovascular responses in vivo" [5]. The proposed circuit: AP/NTS → hypothalamic PVN/ARC/LHA and medullary A5/RVLM/CVLM catecholamine neurons → monosynaptic projections to sympathetic preganglionic neurons in the intermediolateral cell column (IML) → adrenal medulla and heart [5].
- **Direct cardiac mechanism — Pyke et al. 2014** [7]: GLP-1R protein on sinoatrial-node myocytes in human and monkey heart (not ventricular muscle). This supports a direct chronotropic action of GLP-1RAs on the pacemaker, independent of autonomic input.
- **Human heart-rate effect — meta-analytic size.** A 2026 systematic review and pairwise/network meta-analysis (12 trials, non-diabetic overweight/obesity) found vs placebo [6]:

| Agent | Mean difference in HR vs placebo (bpm) | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|
| Liraglutide | +2.37 | 1.86 to 2.89 |
| Semaglutide (s.c.) | +3.35 | 1.69 to 5.01 |
| Oral semaglutide | +4.50 | 3.11 to 5.89 |
| Tirzepatide | +2.05 | 0.96 to 3.13 |
| Retatrutide | +3.46 | 1.74 to 5.18 |
| **Orforglipron** | **+7.30** | 5.48 to 9.12 |
| Total GLP-1RAs | +3.47 | 2.65 to 4.29 |

Network analysis: orforglipron 36 mg showed the largest increase (+9.29 bpm; 95% CI 4.45–13.86); tirzepatide 5 mg the smallest (+0.52) [6]. The Wegovy label reports +1 to +4 bpm for semaglutide 2.4 mg, with 26% of participants showing a ≥20 bpm rise at some visit vs 16% on placebo — the mean conceals a wide spread [21]. Earlier network meta-analysis (Sun et al. 2015, type 2 diabetes) gave the same ~2–3 bpm for long-acting agents [21].
- **Retatrutide time course — DEBATED / dose-dependent.** Phase 2 data showed a dose-dependent rise peaking at **+9 to +10 bpm at week 24**, falling to **+6 to +7 bpm by week 48** (attributed in secondary summaries to the glucagon-receptor component acting on the pacemaker) [22]. This is larger than the 2026 meta-analytic pooled +3.46 bpm for retatrutide [6]; the discrepancy likely reflects dose, time point and population, and should be presented as a dose/time-course question, not as a contradiction to be resolved by picking one number.
- **Heart-rate variability (HRV).** **EMERGING / mixed.** A 12-week wearable study (n=66) found RHR +3.2±0.8 bpm *mediated* by an HRV fall of −6.2±1.4 ms vs control, interpreted as autonomic (reduced vagal) mediation [23]. A 12-week prospective study (n=45, type 2 diabetes) found GLP-1RA therapy reduced SDNN, SDANN, RMSSD, pNN50 and lnHF and raised lnLF and LF/HF in the dapagliflozin-naïve group, with no significant change in the dapagliflozin-exposed group [24]; Kumarathurai et al. reported similar HRV reductions after 12 weeks of liraglutide [24]. However, other studies "have not identified consistent evidence of generalized sympathetic activation or progressive deterioration of cardiac autonomic function" [23]. **Do not state that GLP-1RAs cause sustained sympathetic overdrive in humans — the HRV signal is real but inconsistent, and the outcome trials point the other way (see §4).**

### 3.2 Blood pressure

**ESTABLISHED — net lowering despite higher heart rate.** GLP-1RAs lower systolic BP by ~2–7 mmHg on average (STEP-1 semaglutide 2.4 mg: −6.2 vs −1.1 mmHg placebo at 68 weeks; network meta-analysis in diabetes: −1.84 to −4.60 mmHg) and diastolic by ~2–3 mmHg [6,21]. Mechanisms are partly weight-loss-dependent and partly direct: GLP-1R in proximal tubule → natriuresis; vascular endothelial/smooth-muscle GLP-1R → nitric-oxide vasodilation; anti-inflammatory effects [21]. This is the opposite direction to the acute rodent pressor effect of central GLP-1 [5] — acute central pharmacology and chronic human therapy diverge, a point worth stating explicitly in the survey. A small fraction of patients show modest BP *increases* [21].

### 3.3 Gastric emptying, GI motility, nausea and vomiting

**ESTABLISHED.** GLP-1 delays gastric emptying — the "ileal brake" — via vagal afferent GLP-1R (required for the effect [1]) and via myenteric nitrergic/cAMP pathways reducing antral contractions [19]. Consequences:

- **Satiety:** prolonged gastric distension → sustained vagal satiety signalling.
- **Tachyphylaxis:** the gastric-emptying delay *weakens* with continuous stimulation by long-acting agents, while short-acting agents (exenatide twice daily, lixisenatide) preserve it [19]. This matters for interpreting chronic drug studies.
- **Nausea/emesis — dual peripheral–central mechanism** [19]: (i) peripheral slowing of gastric motility; (ii) direct GLP-1R activation in the **area postrema and NTS** (the chemoreceptor trigger zone / emetic circuitry). "This dual mechanism explains why symptom severity may correlate poorly with the measured degree of gastric-emptying delay and why centrally acting antiemetics are most widely used" [19]. Symptoms are typically mild–moderate, peak during initiation/dose escalation, and attenuate over time; a minority have persistent symptoms and a subset develops gastroparesis [19]. In STEP trials vomiting was reported by ~24.8% on semaglutide vs 6.5% placebo [25]; nausea up to ~32% and diarrhoea up to ~23% in SURPASS/SURMOUNT [19].
- **GIP co-agonism and nausea — EMERGING.** GIP receptors on inhibitory GABA neurons in the AP may attenuate GLP-1RA nausea; tirzepatide produced fewer nausea-like behaviours than semaglutide at matched doses in a 2025 preclinical study, and tirzepatide trials show lower GI burden despite greater weight loss [19,25]. Present as a hypothesis with preclinical support, not as proven in humans.

For the fight-or-flight survey, the salient link is anatomical: **the same dorsal vagal complex (AP/NTS) that triggers vomiting is the first central relay for vagal afferents and the source of central GLP-1** — so nausea, satiety, stress signalling and autonomic outflow share circuitry, and cannot be cleanly separated into "metabolic" vs "stress" effects.

### 3.4 Stress and the HPA axis — the most directly relevant, and most translationally fragile, link

**ESTABLISHED in rodents; DEBATED in humans at therapeutic doses.**

- **Central GLP-1 activates the HPA axis.** Intracerebroventricular GLP-1 activates PVN CRH neurons, elevates ACTH and corticosterone in rats; GLP-1 injected directly into the PVN raises ACTH/corticosterone; CRH-receptor blockade blocks the effect [3,4]. NTS PPG neurons form synaptic contacts with CRH neurons in the PVN [3,4].
- **Site specificity.** Kinzig et al. 2003 [4]: endocrine (ACTH/corticosterone) responses are preferentially driven by GLP-1 in the **PVN**, whereas anxiety-like behaviour (elevated plus maze) is preferentially driven by GLP-1 in the **central nucleus of the amygdala**. GLP-1 antagonists blocked stress-hormone rises to the interoceptive stressor LiCl and blocked both endocrine and anxiety responses to a psychogenic stressor (elevated platform/height) [4].
- **Endogenous GLP-1 is required for normal stress responses.** Conditional deletion of *Glp1r* in the PVN (Sim1 promoter) reduces HPA responses to restraint and hypoxia and attenuates Fos in medial parvocellular PVN neurons; GLP-1R antagonists reduce HPA activation by both psychogenic (elevated platform, restraint) and systemic (LiCl, hypoxia) stressors — unlike NTS catecholamines, which mediate systemic but not psychogenic stress [26]. Daily central GLP-1R antagonist before chronic variable stress blocks stress-induced weight loss and sensitisation of corticosterone responses; daily GLP-1 infusions do the opposite [26].
- **Central GLP-1 is activated by stress.** PPG neurons are activated by interoceptive (LiCl, visceral illness) and psychogenic stressors, and project to the parvocellular PVN [3]. Holt & Trapp (2016) review: "the central GLP-1 system has been found to be activated in response to stressful stimuli… PPG neurons are ideally situated in the brainstem to receive and relay signals of stress" and their projections to the spinal cord suggest "a potential strong link with the sympathetic nervous system" [3].
- **The human contradiction — flag prominently.** The same literature also contains the *opposite* directional claim: that GLP-1RA activation in the PVN *reduces* CRH/cortisol (e.g. in addiction/psychiatry reviews synthesising chronic-treatment and metabolic-improvement effects) [27]. Both can be true at different levels: **acute central GLP-1R stimulation is HPA-activating (rodent, pharmacological doses); chronic GLP-1RA therapy with weight loss and metabolic improvement may normalise a dysregulated HPA axis and lower chronic cortisol** [27,28]. No robust human evidence supports a direct, primary cortisol-modulating effect of GLP-1RAs at therapeutic doses; the clinical significance is "not fully established" [28]. **Do not present either direction as the human fact.** The adrenal cortex itself has no GLP-1R, so any cortisol effect must be central or indirect [3].

### 3.5 Satiety and reward circuits (VTA, nucleus accumbens)

**ESTABLISHED in rodents; EMERGING in humans.**

- NTS PPG neurons project directly to VTA and NAc and release GLP-1 there; blockade of endogenous GLP-1R in VTA or NAc increases palatable high-fat diet consumption, while chemogenetic activation of PPG→VTA projections does the opposite [13]. Exendin-4 injected into VTA or NAc reduces food reward without causing visceral illness (Dickson et al. 2012 [29]).
- **Human fMRI:** van Bloemendaal et al. 2014 (PMID 25071023) — intravenous GLP-1RA (exenatide) reduced activation in insula, amygdala, putamen and orbitofrontal cortex when participants with obesity viewed high-calorie food cues [30]. Ten Kulve et al. 2016: blocking endogenous GLP-1 prevented meal-induced suppression of food-reward brain activation [31]. Tirzepatide fMRI work (2025–26, secondary reports) is described as showing reduced reward-centre activation, but should be cited only from the primary paper once verified — **not yet verified here**.
- **Interpretation for the survey:** GLP-1 dampens *wanting* (mesolimbic dopamine salience), not only *hunger* (hypothalamic homeostasis). Stress, which sensitises amygdala/VTA circuits, therefore interacts with GLP-1 signalling at the same nodes — a plausible mechanism for stress-induced eating and for GLP-1RA effects on craving, but **EMERGING** as a stress-specific (vs food-cue) claim. Reports of anhedonia/emotional blunting on GLP-1RAs exist in pharmacovigilance/secondary literature; EMA reviewed suicidality signals in 2023 without establishing causality [27,28] — **DEBATED**, do not overstate.

### 3.6 How acute stress alters gut GLP-1 secretion and gut permeability

**This is the weakest link in the human literature — state the gap plainly.**

- **Glucocorticoids and L-cells (rodent/in vitro).** Kuckuck et al. 2023 (Obes Rev, DOI 10.1111/obr.13539) [11]: 48-h dexamethasone exposure decreased GLP-1 secretion in murine L-cells *in vitro*; 1-week *in vivo* dexamethasone in rats decreased circulating GLP-1 alongside insulin resistance — interpreted as a possible pathway of "steroid diabetes". **EMERGING; no equivalent human acute-stress experiment was found.**
- **Stress, permeability and motility (established framework).** Acute and chronic stress activate the HPA axis (CRF → cortisol) and sympathetic outflow (norepinephrine). Cortisol modulates GI motility, gastric acid secretion and mucosal barrier protection; CRF directly reduces lower-oesophageal-sphincter pressure, delays gastric emptying and increases visceral sensitivity [18]. Sympathetic norepinephrine inhibits intestinal epithelial proliferation and tight-junction protein synthesis, increasing intestinal permeability under stress — described as "an adaptive response to emergency states" that becomes dysbiotic if prolonged [10]. Stress also inhibits the vagus, removing its anti-inflammatory/permeability-protective brake [9]. The net human picture (cortisol + catecholamines + vagal withdrawal → leakier, dysmotile gut → LPS/cytokine and altered vagal afferent input → NTS/PVN/amygdala) is coherent and widely cited, but the **specific step "acute psychological stress changes gut GLP-1 secretion in humans" has no direct human evidence in the sources retrieved** — it is inferred from dexamethasone/rodent work and from vagal control of L-cell secretion. Flag as **EMERGING / unproven in humans**.
- **Vagal control of secretion.** Vagal (parasympathetic, "rest-and-digest") activity stimulates GLP-1 release; vagotomy blocks fat-induced GLP-1 secretion in animal work (cited in secondary sources — **not independently verified here**, treat as EMERGING). This provides the mechanistic bridge: fight-or-flight (sympathetic dominance + vagal withdrawal) would be *expected* to blunt meal-stimulated GLP-1, but the human experiment is missing.

| Drug | Mechanism | Status (Nov 2026) | Autonomic / CV notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Semaglutide** (Ozempic/Wegovy/Rybelsus; oral Wegovy tablet 25 mg) | GLP-1RA, weekly s.c. or daily oral | Approved | HR +1–4 bpm (label) / +3.35 meta [6]; SBP −6.2 mmHg (STEP-1) [21]; SELECT MACE −20% (HR 0.80) [8] |
| **Tirzepatide** (Mounjaro/Zepbound) | Dual GIP/GLP-1, weekly | Approved | HR +2.05 meta (lowest in class at 5 mg) [6]; lower nausea than semaglutide in trials, hypothesised GIP buffering in AP [19] |
| **Orforglipron** (Foundayo) | Oral small-molecule (non-peptide) GLP-1RA, daily, no food/water timing restrictions | **FDA approved 1 Apr 2026** (first oral non-peptide GLP-1); UK MHRA Aug 2026; CHMP positive opinion Oct 2026, EC decision pending [32] | **Largest HR rise in class**: +7.30 pooled, +9.29 at 36 mg [6]; weight loss ~11–12% at 72 wk (ATTAIN-1) — lower than injectables [32] |
| **Retatrutide** (LY3437943) | Triple GIP/GLP-1/glucagon, weekly | **Investigational — not approved anywhere**; Phase 3 TRIUMPH reported 2026; Lilly planned US filing Q1 2027 [32] | HR +3.46 pooled [6], but phase 2 peak +9–10 bpm wk 24 → +6–7 wk 48 [22]; dysaesthesia, hypotension also reported; weight loss −25.0% at 12 mg/80 wk (TRIUMPH-1, treatment-regimen estimand) vs −3.9% placebo [32] |

**Cardiovascular paradox — ESTABLISHED outcome, DEBATED mechanism.** Every major long-acting GLP-1RA outcome trial reduced MACE despite the HR rise: LEADER (liraglutide) −13%, SUSTAIN-6 (semaglutide) −26%, REWIND (dulaglutide) −12%, SELECT (semaglutide 2.4 mg, obesity without diabetes) −20% (HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.72–0.90; 6.5% vs 8.0% over 39.8 months; n=17,604) [8,21]. A 2025 JACC meta-analysis (21 trials, 99,599 patients, cited in secondary summaries) reported reductions in all-cause death (−12%), CV death (−13%), MI (−15%) and heart failure (−15%), with higher doses linked to more ventricular arrhythmia (1.63×) — **the arrhythmia sub-signal is secondary-source only; verify from the primary meta-analysis before citing in the survey**. The favoured interpretation is that the HR rise (direct SAN effect + modest sympathetic shift) is outweighed by weight loss, BP/natriuresis, glycaemia, anti-inflammatory and vascular effects — but the mediation is not proven, and whether the HR rise is harmless, a marker, or a risk in susceptible subgroups (e.g. HFrEF, where dedicated trials were neutral and HR rise was a safety concern) remains **DEBATED** [21].

**Relevance to stress physiology.** GLP-1RAs are, in effect, a chronic pharmacological probe of the central GLP-1 stress circuit: they deliver sustained GLP-1R activation at circumventricular organs (and, at steady state, PVH/DMH) that endogenous GLP-1, with its 1–2 min half-life, never achieves [1]. Any inference from drug effects back to "what GLP-1 does in stress" must therefore be qualified as pharmacological, not physiological — a distinction Beutler (2026) stresses throughout [1].

## 5. Bidirectional loop — sympathetic activation and cortisol → gut → brain

```
Threat / stressor
  → amygdala + PVN (CRH) ──→ HPA axis → cortisol
  → locus coeruleus / RVLM ──→ sympathetic outflow → NE, adrenaline
        ↓                                    ↓
  NTS PPG neurons activated [3]      gut: ↓ motility, ↓ blood flow,
  (central GLP-1 ↑, HPA/sympathetic ↑) ↓ tight junctions → ↑ permeability,
                                        ↓ SCFA, dysbiosis, ↑ LPS / cytokines
        ↓                                    ↓
  satiety / anorexia, anxiety [4]    vagal afferents (altered GLP-1/PYY/
                                        5-HT/cytokine input) → NTS
                                              ↓
                                   PVN (CRH), amygdala, VTA/NAc, insula
                                   → altered stress reactivity, feeding,
                                      mood, visceral hypersensitivity
                                              ↓
                                   (loop repeats / sensitises)
```

**ESTABLISHED elements of the loop:** stress inhibits the vagus and impairs GI function and microbiota, and is involved in IBS/IBD pathophysiology, both characterised by dysbiosis; low vagal tone is described in IBS and IBD [9]. CRF/CRH receptors mediate stress-related alterations of gut motor function (Taché & Bonaz 2007, cited in [9]). Cortisol weakens tight junctions, alters motility and acid/enzyme secretion, and reshapes microbiota [10,18].

**EMERGING / clinical-correlational:** gut–brain axis in **anxiety and PTSD** — PTSD correlates with gut dysbiosis linked to cognitive dysfunction, and trauma is associated with suppressed vagal tone / lower HRV (secondary summaries report HRV 20–40% below age-matched peers in trauma survivors — **not verified from a primary source here; do not cite as a number**). **IBS and functional dyspepsia** are now conceptualised as disorders of gut–brain interaction with visceral hypersensitivity and altered interoception (over-attention to / misinterpretation of digestive signals), in which stress is a major trigger ( >70% of IBS patients in a 2025 IFFGD survey, secondary source) [33]. The direction of causality (brain→gut vs gut→brain vs shared vulnerability) is **DEBATED**; the most defensible survey statement is bidirectional with central sensitisation as a shared mechanism.

**A specific GLP-1 gap in the loop:** no human study retrieved shows that stress-induced changes in gut permeability or microbiota *change GLP-1 signalling to the brain* in a way that alters stress behaviour. The animal precedent (Grasset et al. 2017: diabetic-mouse dysbiosis induces GLP-1 resistance via an enteric NO-dependent gut–brain mechanism, *Cell Metab* 2017;25:1075–1090) is **EMERGING** and diabetes-specific [34].

## 6. Measurement methods

| What is measured | Method(s) | Pitfalls relevant to fight-or-flight studies |
|---|---|---|
| **Plasma GLP-1** | Immunoassay (RIA/ELISA): *total* GLP-1 (antiserum reacting equally with intact 7–36 and metabolite 9–36) vs *intact* GLP-1 (N-terminal-specific) [14,35]. Human: blood in chilled EDTA tubes + DPP-4 inhibitor, immediate centrifugation, −80 °C; detection ~1–2 pmol/L [35]. LC-MS/MS in some labs. | **Rodents: unreliable without in vivo DPP-4 (± neprilysin) inhibition.** Smits et al. 2024: no GLP-1 rise detectable >15 min after oral glucose in mice without inhibition; intact GLP-1 only rose with sitagliptin (± sacubitril); sample at 5–10 min [14]. Same in rats (2025) [14]. Many older rodent "GLP-1" values are therefore uninterpretable — a critical caveat when citing animal stress studies. |
| **Gastric emptying** | Gold standard: scintigraphy (technetium-labelled meal, % retention). Alternatives: ¹³C-octanoate / ¹³C-acetate breath test; paracetamol (acetaminophen) absorption test (plasma paracetamol AUC as proxy) [35]; wireless motility capsule; ultrasound. | GLP-1RA tachyphylaxis (§3.3) means acute vs chronic measurements differ; paracetamol test is confounded if absorption/metabolism changes with stress. Symptom severity correlates poorly with measured delay [19] — do not use symptoms as a proxy for emptying. |
| **HRV (autonomic balance)** | Time-domain (SDNN, SDANN, RMSSD, pNN50), frequency-domain (lnHF, lnLF, LF/HF) from Holter / 24-h ECG or wearables [23,24]. | LF/HF is *not* a pure sympathetic/parasympathetic ratio; RMSSD/lnHF are the cleaner vagal indices. Wearable HRV (photoplethysmography) is noisier than ECG. GLP-1RA HRV findings are mixed (§3.1) [23]. |
| **Vagal nerve recordings** | Human: microneurography is not feasible for vagal afferents; use HRV, baroreflex sensitivity, gastric myoelectrical activity, or vagal-evoked potentials as proxies. Animal: nodose-ganglion single-unit / patch-clamp (e.g. neuropod co-culture, <100 ms responses [17]), cervical vagus cuff recordings, c-Fos in NTS. | Human vagal *afferent* activity cannot be measured directly — any human claim about "vagal firing" is inferred from HRV or NTS fMRI, not observed. |
| **fMRI of GLP-1 effects** | Food-cue reactivity (BOLD in insula, amygdala, putamen, OFC, hypothalamus, VTA/NAc) with GLP-1RA vs placebo [30]; resting-state connectivity; gastric-distension paradigms. | Small samples, mostly obesity/T2D, acute dosing; BOLD is indirect (neurovascular). Stress + GLP-1 fMRI (e.g. threat cue × drug) is essentially absent — a gap. |
| **HPA / stress readouts to pair with GLP-1** | Salivary/urinary cortisol (diurnal slope, awakening response), plasma ACTH, CRH challenge; dexamethasone suppression. | Cortisol is pulsatile and stress-sensitive to venepuncture itself; pair with HRV and self-report. |
| **Gut permeability / microbiota** | Lactulose/mannitol ratio, serum zonulin, LPS/LBP, faecal SCFA, 16S/metagenomic sequencing. | Zonulin and LPS assays are variable; "leaky gut" is not a standardised clinical diagnosis. |

### 7.2 Open questions and controversies

1. **Physiological vs pharmacological GLP-1.** Does endogenous GLP-1 (gut or brain) regulate stress, or is the stress phenotype only revealed by pharmacological central doses and by receptor knockout/antagonism? Beutler's disconnection finding [1] and the normal body weight of GLP-1R knockouts argue for caution. **DEBATED.**
2. **Direction of the HPA effect in humans.** Acute central stimulation is HPA-activating in rodents [3,4]; chronic therapy may normalise cortisol via metabolic improvement [27,28]. No human dose–response study of GLP-1RA on CRH/ACTH/cortisol dynamics under controlled stress was found. **Unresolved — the single most important gap for this survey's thesis.**
3. **The heart-rate paradox.** Why does a drug that raises HR and (inconsistently) lowers HRV reduce MACE by 20%? Is the HR rise direct (SAN), sympathetic, or both — and does it matter in HFrEF, arrhythmia-prone patients, or with orforglipron/retatrutide's larger rises (+7–10 bpm)? **DEBATED.**
4. **Orforglipron's outsized HR signal.** +7.3 bpm pooled / +9.3 bpm at 36 mg [6] vs +2–3.5 bpm for peptides — is this a small-molecule, CNS-penetration, dose, or population artefact? It rests on few trials (one orforglipron study in the 2026 meta-analysis). **EMERGING — needs replication.**
5. **Does acute psychological stress change gut GLP-1 secretion in humans?** No direct human evidence retrieved; only dexamethasone/rodent data [11]. **Gap.**
6. **GLP-1, reward and mood.** Does mesolimbic dampening [29,30] translate into reduced stress-eating and craving in humans, and at what cost (anhedonia, suicidality signal)? EMA 2023 review found no established causality; FDA January 2026 mood-safety guidance is cited in secondary sources but **not verified from the primary guidance here**. **DEBATED.**
7. **Microbiota → GLP-1 → brain causality in humans.** SCFA→L-cell→vagal signalling is mechanistically coherent [9,20], but human causal evidence (e.g. that changing SCFA changes GLP-1-mediated brain responses) is lacking; Grasset et al.'s GLP-1-resistance-by-dysbiosis finding is mouse/diabetes-specific [34]. **EMERGING.**
8. **Nausea: how much is central (AP/NTS) vs gastric?** The dual-mechanism model [19] explains the poor symptom–emptying correlation, but the relative contributions, and whether GIP co-agonism genuinely buffers nausea in humans, are **DEBATED**.
9. **Sex, age and species differences** in GLP-1R distribution, HPA sensitivity and HR response are barely addressed in the retrieved literature. **Gap.**

### 7.3 Suggested framing for the survey (one paragraph, to paste)

> GLP-1 does not fit neatly on either side of the fight-or-flight divide. Gut GLP-1 is a short-lived postprandial signal that reaches the brain mainly through vagal afferents and circumventricular organs; brain GLP-1, made by NTS preproglucagon neurons, is itself a stress-responsive neuromodulator that activates CRH neurons in the PVN and catecholamine neurons driving sympathetic outflow. The same dorsal vagal complex that mediates nausea also relays gut signals and originates central GLP-1, so satiety, emesis, stress and autonomic control share circuitry. Pharmacological GLP-1 receptor agonists exploit this circuitry at non-physiological duration — raising heart rate by ~2–4 bpm while lowering blood pressure and cardiovascular events — and their effects must not be mistaken for the physiology of the endogenous hormone. Stress, in turn, closes the loop by suppressing vagal tone, increasing gut permeability and (in animal models) reducing GLP-1 secretion, altering the very afferent input on which the brain's interoceptive and stress circuits depend.

# Appendix: Vagus Nerve and GLP-1 — The Vagal–GLP-1 Interface

*Added 16 November 2026. Reference numbers in this appendix (e.g. [1], [2]) refer to the appendix's own Sources list at its end, not to the main report's or the GLP-1 appendix's Sources.*

> **How to use this section.** This is a second add-on to a survey on autonomic fight-or-flight mechanisms that already has a GLP-1 gut–brain appendix. It therefore does **not** repeat general GLP-1 biology (L-cell secretion, pancreatic incretin action, central GLP-1R pharmacology in outline). It goes deeper only on the vagus–GLP-1 interface: which vagal neurons carry GLP-1 signals, in which direction, on what timescale, and where the evidence contradicts itself.
>
> **Confidence flags:** **ESTABLISHED** — replicated across labs/species or consensus in reviews; **EMERGING** — recent, single-lab or preclinical, plausible but unreplicated; **DEBATED** — primary studies directly contradict each other or the interpretation is contested. Almost all mechanistic claims below are from rodents (mouse/rat); human data are flagged where they exist. Sex is a recurrent blind spot: several key anatomy papers used male mice only.

## Summary

The textbook picture — "GLP-1 secreted by L-cells binds GLP-1 receptors on vagal afferents and signals satiety" — is wrong in its details and misleading as a mechanism. **ESTABLISHED:** GLP-1-receptor-expressing (Glp1r+) nodose neurons are predominantly **gastric mechanoreceptors** forming intraganglionic laminar endings (IGLEs), not intestinal nutrient/GLP-1 sensors; the intestinal villus chemosensors are a largely separate **Gpr65+** population. **ESTABLISHED:** vagal GLP-1R signalling contributes to **meal-size control, gastric-emptying delay and the postprandial ("neuro")incretin effect**, but is **dispensable for long-term body-weight regulation and for the anorexia produced by pharmacological GLP-1R agonists**, which act mainly centrally. **ESTABLISHED:** a separate, fast route exists — **neuropod cells** (CCK- and PYY-expressing enteroendocrine cells) form glutamatergic synapse-like contacts with vagal afferents and transmit sugar signals in **<100 ms** — but **DEBATED:** whether GLP-1-containing L-cells do this at all; the best quantitative anatomy says they essentially do not, and communicate **paracrinely**. **ESTABLISHED:** the efferent arm is bidirectional in the other direction too: vagal efferents (via acetylcholine/GRP) drive the **early, proximal-distal phase of GLP-1 secretion** in rodents, and GLP-1 acting in the dorsal vagal complex excites gastric DMV neurons yet produces **gastroinhibition via an inhibitory non-adrenergic, non-cholinergic (NANC) pathway**. **EMERGING:** vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) can raise postprandial GLP-1 in diabetic rats and improve gastric emptying in rodents, but **no human trial has yet shown that VNS/taVNS increases GLP-1 secretion or improves GLP-1RA tolerability** — the relevant trials are protocols only. Clinically, bariatric surgery both massively raises GLP-1 **and** injures vagal afferents; GLP-1RA nausea runs through brainstem CCK neurons in the area postrema/NTS, not simply through peripheral vagal firing; and GLP-1RAs raise resting heart rate and lower HRV in several (but not all) studies without increasing hard vagal-event risk in a large Danish registry.

## 1. Vagal afferent anatomy for GLP-1

### 1.1 The nodose ganglion: ~90% afferent, molecularly parceled

Vagal sensory (afferent) cell bodies sit in the left and right nodose ganglia at the skull base; their peripheral axons innervate viscera and their central axons project to the dorsal hindbrain [1,2]. Afferents outnumber efferents ~9:1. Single-cell/targeted sequencing (Egerod et al. 2018; Kupari et al. 2019; Bai et al. 2019) replaced the old mechano- vs chemo- dichotomy with molecular subtypes. The five gut-relevant markers most used in the literature are *Glp1r*, *Gpr65*, *Oxtr*, *Vip*/*Uts2b* and *Calca* [1,2,3].

**ESTABLISHED — Glp1r+ neurons are gastric IGLE mechanoreceptors.** Williams et al. (2016) generated *Glp1r*-ires-Cre mice and showed, unexpectedly, that Glp1r+ vagal afferents do **not** densely innervate intestinal villi. Instead they form IGLEs in the gastric (and to a lesser extent intestinal) muscle wall and function as gastrointestinal mechanoreceptors, activated by gastric distension in vivo [4]. Bai et al. (2019), using target-guided single-cell RNA-seq of 395 GI-projecting nodose neurons, confirmed that a subset of Glp1r+ neurons are gastric IGLE mechanoreceptors [3]. A 2026 anatomical review concurs: "a majority of gastric IGLEs express *Glp1r*; fewer gastric IGLEs are formed by a distinct *Oxtr*+ population" [5].

**ESTABLISHED — Gpr65+ neurons are the intestinal mucosal nutrient sensors.** The villus-innervating neurons are instead Gpr65+ (with *Sst*/*Calca* in overlapping mucosal clusters). They are insensitive to GLP-1 or CCK, detect serotonin (and, in vivo, broad meal-associated luminal stimuli), and optogenetic activation powerfully blocks gastric contractions without affecting breathing or heart rate [4]. **Implication for the survey:** GLP-1 cannot be assumed to be sensed where it is secreted. Glp1r+ terminals are anatomically distant from most L-cells; Gpr65+ terminals are where the nutrients (and most enteroendocrine cells) are.

**ESTABLISHED — Oxtr+ neurons are a second, more potent IGLE population.** Oxtr+ neurons form IGLEs predominantly in the **proximal intestine** (and stomach/duodenum), with intramuscular arrays (IMAs) also reported, and express *Piezo1/2* mRNA [3,6]. Opto-/chemogenetic stimulation of Oxtr+ IGLEs produced the **most robust suppression of food intake** of any subtype tested — greater than the much larger Glp1r+ IGLE population — and intestinal mechanoreceptor stimulation (but not mucosal-ending stimulation) potently and durably inhibited hypothalamic AgRP hunger neurons [3]. Oxtr+ activation has also been reported to produce a torpor-like hypometabolic/vigilant state with HPA-axis activation — relevant to the fight-or-flight theme, but from a single group and largely preprint-derived, so **EMERGING** [6].

**EMERGING — left/right asymmetry and multi-organ neurons.** Lansbury et al. (2025, RNAscope + retrograde tracing, mice) found *Glp1r*, *Cckar* (CCK-A receptor) and *Npy2r* (PYY Y2 receptor) more highly expressed in the **right** nodose ganglion, whereas *Ntsr1*, *Gpr65* and *Htr3a* were symmetric [7]. Most Glp1r+ neurons co-expressed Cckar and Npy2r (i.e. triple hormone-receptor neurons); nearly all Gpr65+ neurons co-expressed Ntsr1. The anterior stomach was preferentially innervated by the left ganglion, the posterior stomach by the right, and dual retrograde labelling identified triple-receptor neurons innervating **both stomach and colon** — i.e. single vagal afferents sampling the whole GI tract [7]. This challenges the one-neuron-one-organ picture from Bai et al. (only 3–17% double-labelling between any two GI sites) [3]; the discrepancy likely reflects tracer/method differences and is **DEBATED**.

### 1.2 Gut terminal types

| Terminal | Location | Main molecular identity | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mucosal endings** | Lamina propria of villi/crypts; antrum/corpus mucosa | Gpr65+, Vip+/Uts2b+, Calca+ (partly) | Chemosensing: nutrients, serotonin, stroking; **not** innocuous distension [3,4] |
| **IGLEs** (intraganglionic laminar endings) | Between muscle layers, apposed to myenteric ganglia; dense in antrum/fundus/greater curvature, also throughout intestine | Glp1r+ (gastric), Oxtr+ (intestinal) | Tension/stretch mechanoreception; slowly adapting, low-threshold [3,4,5] |
| **IMAs** (intramuscular arrays) | Circular/longitudinal muscle, restricted near pyloric antrum and cardiac sphincter | Oxtr+ (and some Glp1r+) | Putative length/stretch sensing; sparser than IGLEs [3,5,6] |
| **Portal-vein endings** | Along portal vessels/liver hilum | Glp1r protein on terminals (sparse fibres) | Post-absorptive nutrient/hormone sensing; see §2 [8,9] |

Co-expression matters: Glp1r+, Oxtr+ and Vip+ neurons all express *Cckar*, and all four subtypes in Bai et al. express *Htr3a* (5-HT3) [3]. Vagal afferents are therefore **polymodal integrators** (stretch + CCK + serotonin + GLP-1/PYY), not labelled lines for one hormone. **DEBATED:** whether Glp1r+ IGLEs are *also* chemosensors. IGLEs sit on myenteric ganglia and are "likely also chemosensors" linking enteric and brain circuits [5], but direct GLP-1 activation of identified Glp1r+ IGLEs in vivo has not been demonstrated — see §2.3.

### 1.3 Central projections: NTS subnuclei and beyond

**ESTABLISHED:** vagal afferents terminate in the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) of the dorsal vagal complex (DVC), with subnucleus specificity by subtype: Glp1r+ (gastric IGLE) afferents terminate selectively in **ventral NTS, including the DMV region**, whereas Gpr65+ (duodenal mucosal) afferents terminate in more **dorsal NTS — dorsomedial and commissural subnuclei** [4,5]. Both populations also engage the **area postrema (AP)** [1,4]. From NTS, signals relay to hypothalamus (notably AgRP neurons — inhibited by intestinal mechanoreceptors [3]), parabrachial nucleus, and to DMV efferents forming vago-vagal reflexes (see §4).

**ESTABLISHED — a critical dissociation in the NTS itself:** NTS preproglucagon (PPG) neurons, the brain's own GLP-1 source, do **not** receive synaptic input from Glp1r+ vagal afferents; they receive input from **Oxtr+** vagal afferents instead [1]. Peripheral and central GLP-1 systems suppress eating **additively and independently** [1,10]. Do not conflate "vagal GLP-1 signalling" with "NTS GLP-1 neurons" — they are parallel systems that converge only downstream.

### 1.4 How GLP-1 activates nodose neurons

**ESTABLISHED (rat, in vitro / portal):** GLP-1 (10⁻⁹ mol/L) increases cytosolic Ca²⁺ ([Ca²⁺]i) and evokes action potentials in single isolated rat nodose ganglion neurons (Katsurada/Yada) [8]. Intravenous GLP-1 (30–1000 pmol/kg, high-physiological) increased gastric vagal afferent mass activity by **13–52% above basal, peaking 3–5 min** after injection, with no desensitisation on repeated dosing; the GLP-1R antagonist exendin(9-39) abolished the response to 30–300 pmol/kg but did **not** affect the response to gastric distension; GLP-2 had no effect [11]. Intraportal GLP-1 increased hepatic vagal firing and nodose Ca²⁺ influx, and GLP-1R protein is present on nerve terminals in the portal-vein region even though hepatic GLP-1R expression is low/absent [8,9].

**DEBATED / unresolved — the intracellular mechanism.** GLP-1R is a Gs-coupled class-B GPCR (cAMP/PKA in β-cells), but the nodose-specific transduction cascade — which channels close/open, whether cAMP acts via PKA, EPAC or TRP channels, and whether the Ca²⁺ rise is influx vs release — is **not established** in the primary literature reviewed here. Do not import the β-cell cascade into nodose neurons without qualification. Likewise, firing-pattern coding (tonic vs phasic, adaptation) for GLP-1 vs stretch in the *same* Glp1r+ neuron is unmapped: the same cell expresses a stretch transducer (IGLE/Piezo) and a hormone receptor, and how the two inputs summate is an open question (§8).

**DEBATED — does pharmacological exendin-4 activate vagal afferents at all?** Some rodent studies report **no** activation of vagal afferents by exendin-4 ex vivo or in vivo, and preserved anorexia after Glp1r+ afferent ablation (see §2.3) [12,13]. Others report GLP-1-evoked nodose Ca²⁺/firing as above [8,11]. Route, dose, species, and whether the preparation preserves the portal/mucosal terminal environment likely explain the split; the field has not resolved it.

## 2. Vagal afferent GLP-1R function: required vs dispensable

The pattern is consistent once endogenous and pharmacological GLP-1 are separated. **Endogenous, meal-evoked GLP-1 needs vagal GLP-1R for fine postprandial control; pharmacological GLP-1R agonists largely do not.**

### 2.1 What vagal GLP-1R is required / contributes to

| Function | Evidence | Flag |
|---|---|---|
| **Meal-size control** | Krieger et al. (2016): bilateral nodose lentiviral Glp1r knockdown (kd) in rats **increased meal size** [14]. Gastric distension + GLP-1 (inflating stomach formulation) suppresses food intake; effect partly blocked by GLP-1R antagonism and by capsaicin sensory denervation [15]. | ESTABLISHED (rodent) |
| **Gastric-emptying delay** | Same kd: **accelerated gastric emptying** [14]. Imeryüz et al. (1997, cited in [14]): GLP-1 inhibits gastric emptying via vagal afferent-mediated central mechanisms. | ESTABLISHED (rodent) |
| **Postprandial glucose tolerance / neuroincretin effect** | Krieger kd: **elevated postmeal glycaemia, blunted insulin release** — interpreted as loss of a vagal "neuroincretin" effect [14]. Intraportal GLP-1 enhances insulin secretion, attenuated by hepatic vagotomy [8]. In truncally vagotomised humans, gastrointestinal-mediated glucose disposal (GIGD) was **reduced** and DPP-4 inhibition no longer enhanced insulin secretion, despite **higher** peak intact GLP-1 and an **equal** incretin effect (see below) [16]. | ESTABLISHED (rodent); EMERGING/partial (human) |
| **Anorexia of endogenous GLP-1 / nutrient signals** | D-allulose (non-calorific sweetener) suppresses food intake and improves glucose tolerance via vagal afferent GLP-1R signalling; effects diminished by vagotomy, GLP-1R blockade or vagal Glp1r deletion (as reviewed in [15,17]). Capsaicin sensory denervation blocks the anorexia of distension-evoked GLP-1 [15]. | EMERGING (single-lab nutrient models) |

### 2.2 What vagal GLP-1R is dispensable for

| Function | Evidence | Flag |
|---|---|---|
| **Long-term energy balance / body weight** | Krieger kd: **full VAN GLP-1R expression is not necessary for maintenance of long-term energy balance** under normal eating conditions [14]. | ESTABLISHED (rodent) |
| **Anorexia of liraglutide / semaglutide / exendin-4** | Phox2b-Cre; *Glp1r*^fl/fl^ mice (GLP-1R deleted in all vagal sensory afferents): liraglutide **still reduced food intake**; acute exendin-4 anorexia **minimally affected**; chronic dulaglutide weight loss only **partially** prevented [12]. Selective inhibition/ablation of Glp1r+ vagal afferents preserves liraglutide and semaglutide anorexia (but not CCK anorexia) [12]. Nestin-Cre (all-neuron) Glp1r KO, by contrast, **markedly attenuates** liraglutide anorexia — the critical site is central, not vagal [13]. Subdiaphragmatic vagal deafferentation does not prevent sub-chronic liraglutide weight-gain suppression in rats [13]. | ESTABLISHED (rodent) |
| **The incretin effect (human vagotomy)** | Truncally vagotomised subjects (n=16) vs controls (n=10): incretin effect **equal** in both groups despite faster gastric emptying and higher GLP-1 [16]. | ESTABLISHED (human, small n) |

### 2.3 Why the contradictions? A reconciliation

1. **Endogenous vs pharmacological ligand.** Endogenous GLP-1 has a <2-min half-life (DPP-4) and acts locally/paracrinely at vagal terminals before reaching the brain; DPP-4-resistant agonists reach central GLP-1R (via circumventricular organs) directly, bypassing the vagus [8,12]. **2. Dose/route.** Intraperitoneal GLP-1 requires abdominal vagal afferents for anorexia; circulating GLP-1 does not — "intraperitoneal GLP-1 acts like endogenous L-cell GLP-1, acting in a paracrine fashion" [18]. **3. Incomplete vs complete loss.** Lentiviral kd is partial and nodose-restricted; Phox2b-Cre deletion is complete but developmental (compensation possible) and deletes GLP-1R from *all* vagal afferents, including non-GI ones. **4. What is measured.** Meal size and gastric emptying (acute, vagally mediated) dissociate from 24-h food intake and body weight (centrally compensated). **5. Co-transmission.** Glp1r+ neurons co-express Cckar/Npy2r/Htr3a, so CCK/PYY/serotonin can drive the same afferent when GLP-1R is lost [3,7]. Any claim that "the vagus mediates GLP-1 satiety" must specify ligand, route, dose, timescale and readout — otherwise it is unfalsifiable.

## 3. Neuropod cells and millisecond gut–brain signalling

### 3.1 The established circuit (CCK/PYY cells, not L-cells)

**ESTABLISHED (mouse):** Kaelberer et al. (2018, *Science*) showed enteroendocrine cells extend basal processes ("neuropods") that synapse with vagal nodose neurons [19]. Key numbers, verified from the primary paper: ~19% of CCK-expressing enteroendocrine cells contacted Pgp9.5+ sensory fibres; 83% of CCK cells contained synapsin-1; monosynaptic (ΔG-rabies) tracing from colonic PYY cells labelled nodose neurons and, by oral gavage in Cck-Cre mice, NTS neurons — **lumen to brainstem in one synapse**. In organoid–nodose co-culture, photostimulation of a single neuropod cell evoked an action potential in the connected vagal neuron in **<100 ms**. Glutamate was identified as the fast transmitter by iGluSnFR; sucrose/D-glucose vagal firing was abolished by the SGLT1 blocker phloridzin, and nodose neurons alone do not respond to glucose [19].

**ESTABLISHED (mouse):** Buchanan et al. (2022, *Nat Neurosci*) showed duodenal CCK-labelled neuropod cells **discriminate caloric sugar from non-caloric sweetener**: D-glucose via SGLT1 → **glutamate** release; sucralose via sweet-taste receptor T1R3 → **ATP** (purinergic) release. Silencing duodenal CCK neuropods abolished vagal responses to both, and reduced sucrose preference from **82.4% to 44.0%** [20]. This is a *transmitter-code* mechanism: the same cell type uses different transmitters for different stimuli, read out by different vagal afferents.

**EMERGING — other neuropod subtypes.** Enterochromaffin (serotonin) cells form similar neuroepithelial contacts and can drive visceral hypersensitivity; colonic GUCY2C-high neuropod-like cells restrain DRG excitability [21]. Whether these use glutamate, serotonin or ATP as the fast transmitter, and whether they contact vagal vs spinal afferents, is subtype-specific and incompletely mapped.

### 3.2 Do GLP-1-containing L-cells form neuropod synapses? — DEBATED, leaning no

This is the sharpest controversy in the section, and the survey should present both sides:

- **For (morphological):** Bohórquez and colleagues originally described a "neuropod" basal process on GLP-1 cells and reported synaptic markers (synapsin-1, VGLUTs) and rabies tracing consistent with vagal contacts [19,22]. L-cells express generic secretory machinery (*Snap25*, *Nefm*).
- **Against (quantitative anatomy):** Cao, Merchant & Gautron (2024, *Sci Rep*), using Phox2b-Cre-Tomato reporter mice and super-resolution confocal microscopy, found vagal axons made apparent contacts with **<0.5% of GLP-1 cells** in ileum/colon of male mice; PSD-95 immunoreactivity was abundant in the enteric plexus but **virtually absent in the mucosa**; and GLP-1 cells expressed *Snap25*/*Nefm* but **neither** synaptic markers (*Syn1*, *Nrxn2*) **nor** the glutamatergic marker *Slc17a7* (VGLUT1) [22]. Their conclusion: intestinal GLP-1 cells communicate with vagal neurons **primarily through paracrine mechanisms, rather than synaptic-like contacts**. They further note that electron microscopy — the only method that can establish a synapse — has not provided an image of a morphologically identifiable enteroendocrine–vagal synapse, and that only a small subset of Glp1r+ vagal afferents innervate the intestinal mucosa where L-cells reside [22].

**Assessment:** the fast (millisecond, glutamatergic) gut–brain route is established for **CCK duodenal and PYY colonic** neuropods; for **GLP-1 L-cells it is not established and the best quantitative evidence is against a synaptic route**. GLP-1's vagal action is better modelled as **paracrine diffusion over minutes** to nearby Glp1r+ mucosal/portal terminals (and to Gpr65+ terminals via other L-cell co-transmitters such as ATP [22]), not as a millisecond synapse. Any figure that draws a GLP-1 L-cell synapsing on the vagus should be flagged as hypothetical. Note the caveat: Cao et al. studied **male mice, lower intestine only**; duodenal/jejunal L-cells (fewer, but present) and females were not examined — a genuine gap, not a refutation-proof.

### 3.3 Timescale synthesis (useful for the fight-or-flight framing)

| Route | Latency | Transmitter | Cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neuropod synapse | **milliseconds (<100 ms)** | Glutamate (sugar) / ATP (sweetener) | CCK duodenal, PYY colonic neuropods |
| Paracrine GLP-1 → vagal terminal | **minutes** (peak vagal firing 3–5 min after IV GLP-1 [11]) | GLP-1 (7-36) | L-cell → Glp1r+ mucosal/portal afferent |
| Endocrine GLP-1 → brain | **minutes**, limited by DPP-4 (<2 min half-life) | GLP-1 | L-cell → AP/NTS via blood |
| Pharmacological GLP-1RA → brain | **hours–days** (stable, DPP-4-resistant) | Agonist | → central GLP-1R, largely vagus-independent |

The millisecond route is therefore **not a GLP-1 route** on current evidence — it is a *parallel* fast channel that lets the brain know *what* (sugar vs sweetener) arrived before hormones report *how much*.

## 4. Vagal efferent side

### 4.1 DMV output: two opposing gastric pathways

**ESTABLISHED:** Preganglionic parasympathetic motor neurons in the **dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus (DMV)**, ventral to the NTS in the DVC, send cholinergic axons that synapse on enteric (myenteric) neurons, not directly on muscle/glands [5,23]. Two postganglionic pathways originate there: (i) an **excitatory cholinergic** pathway (ACh → muscarinic receptors) that increases gastric motility, tone and secretion; (ii) an **inhibitory NANC** pathway (nitric oxide, VIP) that decreases motility/tone [23]. Vago-vagal reflexes (afferent → NTS → DMV) therefore can produce either effect depending on which DMV subpopulation and postganglionic pathway is recruited.

**ESTABLISHED — GLP-1's gastric action is vagally mediated and NANC-dependent.** Holmes et al. (2009): GLP-1 concentration-dependently **excited a subpopulation of identified gastric-projecting DMV neurons** (mimicked by exendin-4, antagonised by exendin-9-39); microinjection of exendin-4 into the DVC **decreased gastric tone** concentration-dependently; this gastroinhibition was **unaffected by the muscarinic agonist bethanechol** but **abolished by the NOS inhibitor L-NAME or by bilateral vagotomy** [23]. Interpretation: GLP-1 excites DMV neurons that drive the inhibitory NANC input to the stomach — excitation in the brainstem produces inhibition in the gut. GLP-1 also excites pancreas-projecting DMV neurons (insulin secretion arm) [23]. Gastric acid secretion, by contrast, is driven by the excitatory cholinergic pathway (ACh → M3 on parietal cells, plus histamine/gastrin amplification); GLP-1's net effect on the stomach is inhibitory, not secretory.

### 4.2 Cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway (CAP)

**ESTABLISHED (rodent):** The "inflammatory reflex" / CAP (Tracey group): cytokines/LPS activate vagal **afferents** → NTS integration (muscarinic-dependent) → vagal **efferents** from DMV → coeliac–superior mesenteric plexus → splenic nerve (noradrenaline → β2-adrenergic receptors on choline-acetyltransferase+ T cells) → acetylcholine → **α7 nicotinic ACh receptor (α7nAChR) on macrophages** → inhibition of NF-κB nuclear translocation and activation of JAK2/STAT3 → selective suppression of TNF, IL-1, HMGB1 (anti-inflammatory cytokines spared) [24,25]. Wang et al. (2003): α7 is **required** — electrical VNS inhibits TNF synthesis in wild-type but **fails in α7-deficient mice**; α7 agonists/nicotine/ACh inhibit TNF from endotoxin-stimulated human macrophages [24]. Vagotomy or α7 disruption exacerbates TNF responses and endotoxin lethality [25].

**DEBATED / nuance — the splenic relay and human translation.** The vagus does not directly innervate the spleen in most anatomical accounts; the relay via the sympathetic splenic nerve is a rodent circuit whose human anatomy/physiology is contested, and α7's ion-channel activity appears absent in leukocytes (metabotropic-like signalling is assumed) [25]. Clinical VNS anti-inflammatory trials (e.g. rheumatoid arthritis) are small and preliminary. For this survey: present the CAP as a rodent-established reflex with a **specific molecular effector (macrophage α7nAChR)** but **unproven human therapeutic translation**.

**No established direct GLP-1 → CAP link.** GLP-1RAs have anti-inflammatory associations and GLP-1 can modulate immune cells, but no primary study reviewed here shows GLP-1 activating the CAP or requiring α7nAChR. Do not merge the two pathways: GLP-1's vagal actions are afferent/DMV-gastric; the CAP is an afferent→efferent immune reflex. Their intersection (e.g. whether GLP-1RA nausea/inflammation involves CAP) is speculative.

### 4.3 Efferent vagal control of L-cell GLP-1 secretion (the proximal-distal loop)

**ESTABLISHED (rodent) / DEBATED (human):** GLP-1 secretion is biphasic: an early peak at 5–15 min occurs **before nutrients reach distal L-cells**, implying a proximal signal. Rocca & Brubaker (1999, rat in situ gut): duodenal fat infusion (isolated segment, no luminal progression to ileum) increased GLP-1/gut glucagon-like immunoreactivity; **bilateral subdiaphragmatic vagotomy completely abolished** the fat-induced rise; **stimulation of the distal coeliac branch of the vagus significantly stimulated** GLP-1/gGLI secretion; physiological GIP infusion increased GLP-1 secretion, and **selective hepatic-branch vagotomy abolished** that GIP effect [26]. Mechanism: GIP (from duodenal K-cells) → vagal afferents → vagal efferents/enteric neurons → **acetylcholine and gastrin-releasing peptide (GRP)** → L-cells. ACh stimulates human and rodent L-cells in vitro; atropine or the M1 antagonist pirenzipine blocks duodenal-nutrient-evoked GLP-1 secretion in rodents; **atropine prevents GLP-1 secretion after nutrient ingestion in humans** [26,27].

**The human discordance:** **GIP does not stimulate GLP-1 secretion in humans in vivo** [26,27]. So the rodent GIP→vagus→GLP-1 loop cannot be assumed in humans; the human proximal-distal loop may use a different proximal signal (or direct neural/GRP signalling without GIP). Flag as **DEBATED (species difference)**: vagal efferent control of L-cells is established in rodents and supported by atropine data in humans, but the GIP link is rodent-specific on current evidence. Conversely, selective vagal deafferentation (capsaicin, rat) **increased** baseline and OGTT-evoked GLP-1 and insulin and improved glucose tolerance [28] — suggesting vagal afferents can also exert tonic *inhibitory* restraint on GLP-1 secretion, a finding that complicates any simple "vagus stimulates GLP-1" slogan.

## 5. Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS)

### 5.1 Invasive cervical VNS

Approved for epilepsy and treatment-resistant depression; gut/metabolic use is investigational. **EMERGING (rodent):** (i) Auricular VNS (aVNS) in type-2 diabetic rats **elevated postprandial plasma GLP-1 at 30 min** vs sham and improved hyperglycaemia, attributed to enhanced incretin release and vagal efferent activity (Li et al., conference abstract, *Gastroenterology* 2024) [29] — abstract-level evidence, not a full peer-reviewed paper. (ii) A battery-free gastric VNS device in rats reduced body weight by ~38% and **increased plasma GLP-1 and PYY** (as summarised in a 2025 review [30]) — primary data not verified here; treat as **EMERGING/unverified**. (iii) Vagal blockade (vBloc/ Maestro, FDA-approved 2015) produced 24.4% excess weight loss at 12 months vs 15.9% sham in the ReCharge trial [30] — i.e. *blocking* vagal signalling can also reduce weight, underscoring that "more vagus = more satiety" is false; afferent vs efferent, tonic vs phasic, and fibre-type selectivity matter.

### 5.2 Transcutaneous auricular VNS (taVNS)

taVNS stimulates the auricular branch (Arnold's nerve, cymba conchae) → jugular-nodose ganglion → NTS. **ESTABLISHED (rodent):** taVNS improves gastric emptying and visceral hypersensitivity in a neonatal-iodoacetamide rat model of functional dyspepsia, **comparable to invasive subdiaphragmatic VNS**, via the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway (α7nAChR/NF-κB) [29,31]. **EMERGING (human, functional dyspepsia):** a scoping review (search to Dec 2025) found 4 RCTs (n=480) showing improved dyspeptic symptoms, gastric accommodation and slow-wave normalisation vs sham, sustained 4–12 weeks, but with heterogeneous parameters (10–30 Hz, 0.5–10 mA, 2–4 weeks) and low–moderate risk of bias [31]. Candidate mechanisms include autonomic/cholinergic modulation, α7/NF-κB anti-inflammation with duodenal barrier restoration, HPA-axis inhibition, and rebalancing of brain–gut peptides **including GLP-1** — but GLP-1 was a listed biomarker/mechanism, not a demonstrated mediator [31].

**EMERGING (human, obesity/NTS):** a 2026 review argues taVNS may restore NTS-mediated satiety by **upregulating satiety-promoting GLP-1ergic NTS neurons** and attenuating aberrant TH+ catecholaminergic NTS activity — based on rodent data; the authors flag unstandardised parameters and no large long-term RCTs [32].

**No results yet — the GLP-1RA combination trials:** (i) taVNS + semaglutide in T2DM with GI side effects: randomised, sham-controlled protocol, n=60, 12 weeks, **primary outcome Rhodes Index of Nausea/Vomiting/Retching** (Front Neurol 2025) [33]. (ii) taVNS for metformin GI intolerance: n=60 pilot, biomarkers include **GLP-1, 5-HT, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α** [34]. Neither has reported outcomes. **Do not cite taVNS as an established adjunct to GLP-1RA therapy.**

|---|---|---|

## 6. Clinical relevance

### 6.1 Vagotomy (human)

Truncal vagotomy with pyloroplasty (n=16) vs matched controls (n=10), OGTT ± vildagliptin (DPP-4 inhibitor) and isoglycaemic IV glucose: **GIGD reduced**; gastric emptying **faster** and unaffected by DPP-4 inhibition; peak intact GLP-1 **higher**; early GIP response higher; **incretin effect equal**; DPP-4 inhibition enhanced insulin in controls but **not** in vagotomised subjects; DPP-4 inhibition further suppressed glucagon in controls and tended to normalise it in vagotomised subjects [16]. Interpretation (authors): part of physiological GLP-1's effect involves vagal transmission, but the incretin effect per se survives vagotomy — consistent with §2. In rats, truncal vagotomy lowers food intake/body weight yet **blunts the anorectic response to acute GLP-1** and raises GLP-1-evoked leptin, suggesting a vagus-dependent GLP-1–leptin axis [36] — single study, **EMERGING**.

### 6.2 Bariatric surgery

**ESTABLISHED:** Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) massively increases postprandial GLP-1 (up to ~6-fold in some series) and PYY via accelerated nutrient delivery to distal L-cells (hindgut hypothesis), with more moderate increases after sleeve gastrectomy (SG); GLP-1-receptor-deficient animals still lose weight after RYGB, and exendin(9-39) worsens glycaemia post-RYGB without returning it to baseline — **GLP-1 is important but not the sole mediator** [17,37].

**EMERGING — RYGB injures the vagus it relies on:** In obese male mice, RYGB induced **Atf3 mRNA (axonal-injury marker) in a subset of vagal sensory neurons**, significantly **reduced the proportion of Glp1r-expressing vagal afferents and total nodose *Glp1r* mRNA**, and nerve-transection experiments replicated these changes — i.e. axonal injury alone may account for them. Paradoxically, acute exendin-4 **enhanced vagal firing more** in RYGB (and vagal-injury) animals than in controls: **vagal neuropathy with reduced receptor expression but heightened GLP-1 sensitivity** [38]. Anatomically, RYGB transects vagal branches to the gastric remnant (pouch vagotomised, pouch fibres intact); SG resects fundic vagal innervation and transects distal gastric vagal endings, accelerates gastric emptying, and increases GLP-1/PYY while markedly reducing ghrelin [37,39]. In mice, **TGR5 (bile-acid receptor) signalling is required** to maintain VSG benefits on weight, glucose tolerance, hepatic steatosis and GLP-1 secretion [39] — bile-acid/vagal interaction, **EMERGING**.

**DEBATED — does endogenous GLP-1 still suppress eating after RYGB?** Svane et al.: exendin(9-39) **increased food intake before RYGB but not after**; post-operatively GLP-1 *and* PYY are hugely elevated, and exendin(9-39) itself paradoxically raises them further (paracrine negative feedback), confounding the blockade test [17,37]. A trial adding truncal vagotomy to RYGB (VagusSx, NCT07278115) is testing whether vagal interruption *improves* diabetes remission — i.e. the field is unsure whether post-RYGB vagal signalling helps or harms [40]. Do not present post-bariatric GLP-1 as simply "more hormone, more vagal satiety".

### 6.3 GLP-1RA nausea and the vagal emetic pathway

**ESTABLISHED:** Nausea (~40–50% on semaglutide) and vomiting (~25%) are the commonest dose-limiting effects, worst in the 48 h after dose escalation, habituating over 2–4 weeks at a stable dose [41]. Mechanism is **dual**: (i) **central** — GLP-1R in the DVC (NTS + AP); the AP lacks a normal blood–brain barrier (chemosensory trigger zone), so circulating agonists directly activate it; (ii) **peripheral** — GLP-1R on vagal afferents from stomach/intestine, amplified by slowed gastric emptying → gastric distension → increased vagal afferent firing [41]. **ESTABLISHED (mouse):** CCK-expressing NTS/AP neurons (CCK^AP/NTS^) are **required** for the full anorectic and body-weight-lowering effects of exendin-4 *and* for GLP-1RA-induced conditioned taste avoidance (the rodent nausea proxy); they express high *Glp1r* but no *Gipr*; GIP-receptor co-activation (via neighbouring GABAergic interneurons) dampens a subset of CCK^AP/NTS^ neurons and **selectively reduces conditioned taste avoidance without losing anorexia** — a mechanistic rationale for why dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists (tirzepatide) may be better tolerated [42]. Note the dissociation from §1.3: the nausea-relevant CCK neurons are *postsynaptic* brainstem targets of GLP-1RAs, not the vagal afferents themselves; and NTS PPG (central GLP-1) neurons are dispensable for exendin-4 anorexia [1,42].

### 6.4 Vagal tone / HRV in GLP-1RA therapy, and dysautonomia

**DEBATED — HRV direction and meaning.** GLP-1RAs as a class **increase resting heart rate** (~2–3 bpm) [43]. A 12-week prospective observational study (n=45, T2DM on GLP-1RA): the control (dapagliflozin-naïve) group showed significant **reductions in SDNN, SDANN, RMSSD, pNN50 and lnHF, with rises in lnLF and lnLF/lnHF** — i.e. reduced vagal HRV indices; the dapagliflozin-exposed group showed no significant HRV change (authors: hypothesis-generating, needs larger prospective confirmation) [43]. **Contradicting** this, a small exenatide-ER study (n=28, 3–6 months) found LF/HF **fell** from 3.05±0.4 to 1.64±0.2 lying (and 4.56 to 2.24 standing), interpreted as a **relative increase in vagal influence** on the heart, with HR rise unrelated to sympathetic tone [44]. Differences in drug (short- vs long-acting), duration, posture, HRV metric and the fact that HR rise itself compresses HRV indices make the literature uninterpretable as a single "GLP-1RA lowers vagal tone" claim. **Do not use HRV as a proxy for gut vagal tone** — cardiac vagal and gut vagal outflows are separately regulated (DMV vs nucleus ambiguus).

**ESTABLISHED (registry) — no excess hard vagal events:** In Danish nationwide registers (T2DM and obesity cohorts, GLP-1RA initiators vs SGLT2-inhibitor active controls, 2010–2022), standardised 1-year absolute risk of syncope was **0.58% (GLP-1RA) vs 0.56% (SGLT2i)**; risks of fracture, bradyarrhythmia and cardiac device implantation were equally low (risk ratios 0.90, 0.97, 0.86) [45]. The authors stress direct enhancement of cardiac vagal activity by GLP-1RAs "has not been conclusively demonstrated in humans" and vagal-mediated effects should be considered biologically plausible rather than established [45].

**EMERGING / unproven — dysautonomia implications.** Diabetes with cardiac autonomic neuropathy, gastroparesis and other dysautonomias (including POTS-like presentations) plausibly interact with GLP-1RA therapy in both directions — blunted endogenous vagal GLP-1 signalling, exaggerated distension/nausea from delayed emptying, and orthostatic symptoms amplified by nausea/dehydration — but **no trial has tested GLP-1RAs in dysautonomia cohorts**, and GLP-1RAs have not been studied in severe gastroparesis and are not recommended there [41,45]. For the survey's fight-or-flight framing, the defensible statement is: chronic sympathetic dominance / low HRV is a *risk marker* in these patients, GLP-1RAs modestly raise HR and (in some studies) lower HRV indices, yet hard vagal-event risk is not increased in registry data; any claim that GLP-1RAs "improve" or "worsen" dysautonomia is unsupported.

## 8. Open questions / controversies

1. **Is there a GLP-1 synapse?** Does any enteroendocrine subtype co-releasing GLP-1 (e.g. duodenal L/K-cells, or GLP-1+/CCK+ cells) form a functional glutamatergic neuropod synapse, or is the CCK/PYY neuropod circuit GLP-1-independent? Needs EM-level evidence, female animals, and upper-gut L-cells — Cao et al. examined male lower gut only [22].
2. **What transduces GLP-1 in nodose neurons?** Gs→cAMP is assumed, not shown, in nodose cells; the channel(s) and Ca²⁺ source, and how stretch (Piezo/IGLE) and GLP-1R inputs summate in the same neuron, are unknown.
3. **Why does exendin-4 activate nodose neurons in some preparations and not others?** Route/dose/species/terminal preservation — a systematic replication is needed before any claim about "GLP-1RA vagal activation" is made.
4. **Endogenous GLP-1 after RYGB:** is the loss of exendin(9-39) effect on food intake post-RYGB real (GLP-1 no longer needed) or an artefact of PYY co-elevation and feedback [17,37]? And does RYGB vagal neuropathy with *heightened* exendin-4 sensitivity [38] help or hinder long-term weight maintenance?
5. **Human proximal-distal loop:** which proximal signal drives early GLP-1 secretion in humans, if not GIP? Atropine data implicate cholinergic signalling, but the afferent trigger is unidentified [26,27].
6. **taVNS + GLP-1RA:** do the ongoing sham-controlled trials [33,34] show any effect on nausea, GLP-1 levels or glycaemia? Until they report, taVNS is a hypothesis, not an adjunct.
7. **HRV paradox:** why do some GLP-1RA studies show reduced HRV indices and others a relative vagal increase [43,44]? Drug, duration, posture and HR-confounding need a pre-registered, standardised HRV study — and cardiac HRV must not be conflated with gut vagal tone.
8. **Sex differences:** Oxtr+ IGLE density and oxytocin efficacy differ by sex [6]; PPG-neuron stress effects are sex-dependent [10]; most anatomy (including Cao et al.) used males only. Female vagal–GLP-1 physiology is systematically under-studied.
9. **The CAP in humans:** does the rodent splenic relay exist in humans, and does GLP-1RA anti-inflammation involve vagal/α7 mechanisms at all? Currently no direct evidence.
10. **Fight-or-flight integration (for the parent survey):** intestinal mechanoreceptor (Oxtr+) activation produces vigilance/HPA activation and hypometabolism [6], while NTS PPG neurons mediate stress hypophagia [10] — the vagus–GLP-1 interface is therefore not only a satiety system but a *threat/interoception* system. Whether GLP-1RAs alter stress reactivity via this circuit in humans is untested.
